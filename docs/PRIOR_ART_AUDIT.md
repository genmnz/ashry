# Prior-art audit: "stoichiometry-grounded, sequence-informed producibility index for recombinant food proteins in *K. phaffii* and *T. reesei*"

Adversarial audit. Date: 2026-11-18. Screened ~220 records (Europe PMC REST, Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, bioRxiv API, GitHub search, web search). Companion machine-readable list: `data/literature/papers_priorart.tsv`.

**Verdict in one line: the method is not novel; the method applied to *this* target panel, validated against *measured* literature titers, with an explicit gap decomposition against the stoichiometric ceiling, is novel but narrow. Steps (1) and (2) as written are already published — twice in yeast, once in exactly *K. phaffii*, and once under exactly the word "producibility" in exactly *T. reesei*.**

## 2. Verdict: what is novel, what is not

### Not novel — cut or demote to Methods

1. **"Compute each protein's exact metabolic cost from its amino-acid composition."** Settled since Akashi & Gojobori 2002; done in a GEM with glycerol by Kaleta 2013; done per-protein from the same seven sequence features by Feizi 2013; done in *K. phaffii* with iMT1026v3 by Acharya et al. 2026. Claiming this as a contribution will not survive review. It is a **Methods subsection**.
2. **"ATP/NADPH burden per protein."** Same. Feizi 2013 and Gutierrez 2020 both report per-protein cofactor budgets.
3. **"A sequence-feature panel."** Every feature is an existing public tool, and the PTM/disorder/disulfide subset is exactly Feizi's PSIM plus SECRiFY's disorder finding. Assembly is not invention.
4. **"Says which bottleneck binds for each protein."** pcSecYeast 2022 already does per-protein bottleneck attribution (secretory vs metabolic) across eight proteins. Acharya 2026 does bottleneck identification in *K. phaffii*. Do not frame this as the novelty.
5. **"A producibility index for *T. reesei* from sequence."** Dykstra, Rousu & Arvas 2017 published this under that exact word for that exact host. You must cite it and state your delta. Failing to cite it reads as ignorance of the field.
6. **The expected ranking itself** (α-La/β-Lg easy → ovalbumin → LegH → casein hardest). Already in print, qualitatively, in `10.1111/1541-4337.70618` and restated across the 2024–2026 reviews. If your index reproduces it, you have validated a known ordering; if it contradicts it, you must explain why.
7. **A new per-kg techno-economic model.** Humbird 2021 and the GFI/industry models exist and already use stoichiometric yields. Parameterise, do not re-derive.

### Genuinely novel — the defensible core

1. **Target panel.** No published stoichiometric-cost or secretory-cost work covers food proteins. Everything cited above runs on α-amylase, insulin, HSA, hGCSF, transferrin, haemoglobin, VHH, RBD, IgG1, or native cellulases. Casein, β-lactoglobulin, α-lactalbumin, ovalbumin, leghemoglobin and lactoferrin are an empty cell. This is real but thin on its own — it is "same method, new substrate list", the weakest kind of novelty.
2. **Titer-validated regression.** This is the strongest piece. pcSecYeast and Gutierrez regress/attribute features against **model-simulated** productivity. Lin 2014 regresses against measured titers but only for CHO mAb CDR3s. Tortajada 2012 regresses productivity on ATP flux but for one strain as a soft-sensor. **Nobody has fit a cross-protein, cross-host model to the published experimental titer record for K. phaffii / T. reesei.** That requires the curated titer database you are building (`docs/TITER_DB_SPEC.md`) — the database is itself a defensible artifact and arguably more citable than the index.
3. **"Distance from the stoichiometric ceiling" gap decomposition.** Computing the maximum theoretical g protein per g glycerol/methanol per protein and then expressing each measured titer as a fraction of that ceiling, with the residual attributed to specific sequence features, is a framing I could not find anywhere. Pentjuss 2023 — the one paper closest to doing it for legH in iMT1026 — **verifiably does not report any product yield ceiling**. Gutierrez 2020 computes "maximum productivities" but does not decompose measured-vs-ceiling gaps. This is the only part of the claim with a clean mechanism-level story.
4. **Cross-host comparability** (*K. phaffii* vs *T. reesei* on a common cost basis). Minor, but no one has put the two side by side on a shared stoichiometric metric.

### The sharpest honest framing

> *Published per-protein cost and secretory-burden models (Feizi 2013; Gutierrez 2020; pcSecYeast 2022) predict production capability for pharmaceutical proteins and validate against simulations or single-product experiments. We ask instead how far the published food-protein titer record sits from the stoichiometric ceiling of its host. We compute, for each of N food proteins, the maximum theoretical g protein per g glycerol or methanol in iMT1026v3 / the T. reesei GEM; we assemble a curated titer database of M measurements; and we show that the realised fraction of the ceiling spans X orders of magnitude and is explained not by metabolic cost — which varies less than Yx across the panel — but by [disorder / glycosylation load / disulfide count]. The contribution is the gap decomposition and the titer database, not the cost calculation.*

Two load-bearing consequences of this framing:

- **The index must be allowed to fail.** The most likely and most publishable result is that **metabolic cost is NOT the binding constraint** for food proteins — amino-acid cost varies little across these sequences, while titers vary 100-fold. Pre-register that as a possible finding. "Cost is not the bottleneck, and here is the stoichiometry that proves it" is a stronger, harder-to-scoop result than a scoring tool.
- **Call it something other than "producibility index".** The word is taken (Dykstra 2017), and "index" invites the fatal "you fit a composite score to ~15 points" objection. Prefer *ceiling-gap analysis*, *stoichiometric headroom*, or *cost-versus-realised-titer decomposition*.

## 3. The three strongest objections, and whether they are answerable

### Objection 1 (fatal if unaddressed): "Feizi 2013, Gutierrez 2020 and pcSecYeast 2022 already compute per-protein cost from exactly your feature set. Acharya et al. 2026 already did it in *K. phaffii* with iMT1026v3. What is left?"

**Answerable — but only by conceding steps (1) and (2).** The honest answer: nothing in the cost calculation is new, and the write-up must say so in the first paragraph of the Methods, with all four citations. What survives is the **measured-titer target variable** and the **ceiling-gap decomposition** on a food-protein panel. Any draft that presents the cost pipeline as a contribution will be rejected by anyone who has read the Nielsen-lab line of work.

**Risk flag:** Acharya et al. is **unpublished**, is from a well-resourced lab (Love, MIT), its code went public on 2026-10-29, and the same group published `10.1186/s12934-026-03009-7` in 2026 on K. phaffii secretion-gene deletions. If they add food proteins or a titer regression in a Paper 2, the remaining novelty narrows further. Treat this as a race and watch that repo.

### Objection 2 (hardest to answer): "You are fitting a model to a handful of heterogeneous literature titers. That is not a predictive model, it is overfitting to a confounded dataset."

**Only partly answerable.** The published titer record for these proteins is small (order 10–100 measurements), and the dominant variance is almost certainly **not** protein identity but strain, promoter, copy number, signal peptide, cultivation mode, carbon feed and quantification method — the exact covariates your own `TITER_DB_SPEC.md` is right to record. With ~6 target proteins, a multi-feature regression has more parameters than informative points.

Honest mitigations, in order of strength:
1. **Do not fit a multi-feature predictive model at all.** Report the ceiling-gap per protein as a descriptive quantity, with a mixed-effects model where process covariates are random effects and protein identity is the fixed effect of interest. State the confidence intervals and let them be wide.
2. Expand the training set beyond food proteins to all *K. phaffii* / *T. reesei* secreted recombinant proteins with published titers (hundreds of measurements), then treat the food proteins as held-out predictions. This is the single highest-value design change available, and it is also the design that makes the Dykstra 2017 comparison explicit.
3. Report leave-one-protein-out performance and a permutation null. If the model does not beat "predict the panel mean", say so.

An examiner can and will press this. There is no framing that makes a 6-protein regression defensible; only a larger training set or an abandonment of the regression does.

### Objection 3: "Your index's output is the ranking already published qualitatively in the 2026 milk-protein review — you have quantified received wisdom."

**Answerable, and this one is winnable.** Three lines of reply: (a) the review's ranking is argued from PTM dependency alone, while the stoichiometric result may **disagree** with it — caseins are cheap, disordered, short and cysteine-free, so by metabolic cost and folding load they should be *easy*, and their difficulty is PTM/functionality, not production; demonstrating that split quantitatively is a genuine result; (b) a ceiling-gap number is actionable (it says how much headroom strain engineering has) where a tractability adjective is not; (c) the review itself names co-optimising yield, folding fidelity and PTM accuracy as an open frontier, which is a citable invitation.

**Fourth objection worth pre-empting** (an examiner who knows constraint-based modelling will raise it): *"Why a bespoke index instead of an ecModel/ME-model of K. phaffii, which would give cost, proteome allocation and bottlenecks in one object?"* Answer honestly: because no proteome-constrained secretory K. phaffii model exists yet, and because the measured-titer comparison is the point. Note also `10.1002/bit.26380` — predicted targets in K. phaffii are sensitive to the assumed biomass composition — so every cost number needs a sensitivity analysis over biomass composition and growth rate, or it will be dismissed as a point estimate with hidden error bars.

## 4. Answer to the framing question posed by the coordinator

> Given Feizi 2013 and Irani 2015 exist, is the defensible novelty (i) the food-protein panel, (ii) the titer-validated regression, (iii) the ceiling-gap decomposition, or (iv) some combination — and what would you cut?

**(iii) + (ii), with (i) as the setting rather than the claim. Cut the index framing entirely.**

- **(iii) ceiling-gap decomposition — keep as the headline.** It is the only element I could not find published, and Pentjuss 2023 is verifiably silent on product yield ceilings, so the legH/food-protein ceiling numbers are unoccupied. It is also the element that is robust to a small dataset, because it is a stoichiometric calculation plus a descriptive comparison, not a fitted model.
- **(ii) titer-validated regression — keep, but demote to a validated descriptive analysis and only if the training set is widened beyond the food panel.** As a 6-protein predictive model it is indefensible (Objection 2). The **curated titer database** is the durable artifact here and should be released and cited in its own right.
- **(i) food-protein panel — keep as the setting, not the novelty.** "Same method, new substrate list" is the weakest available claim and invites the comparison to Acharya et al. Say plainly: these are the proteins that matter commercially and none of the existing cost models cover them.
- **Cut:** the word "producibility" (taken by Dykstra 2017 for exactly *T. reesei*); any claim to novelty in the cost calculation, the ATP/NADPH accounting, the sequence-feature panel, or per-protein bottleneck attribution; and any new per-kg techno-economic model.

**Also do these, concretely:**
- Cite Feizi 2013, Gutierrez 2020, pcSecYeast 2022, Dykstra 2017, Arvas 2016, Kaleta 2013 and Acharya 2026 (as an unpublished preprint/code reference) in the first Methods paragraph. The audit's biggest finding is not a missing gap — it is a **citation gap**.
- Verify whether Irani 2016 (`10.1002/bit.25863`) reports a g-per-g-substrate yield anywhere, including supplementary. **I could not: the paper is paywalled and no OA full text was reachable in this audit.** If it does, the ceiling claim weakens and must be rephrased as "first for food proteins on glycerol and methanol".
- Monitor `github.com/RaghavAcharya/kphaffii-metabolic-bottlenecks` and Europe PMC for the Acharya et al. publication.

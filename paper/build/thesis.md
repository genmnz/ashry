## Abstract

Precision fermentation — programming a microorganism by recombinant DNA to secrete a protein that an animal would otherwise have made — has moved from laboratory demonstration to supermarket shelf in under a decade. Bovine β-lactoglobulin from *Trichoderma reesei* and soy leghemoglobin from *Komagataella phaffii* are both cleared for sale in the United States. Yet the field's own framing of its central problem has remained qualitative: proteins are said to be hard to make because they fold poorly, because they require post-translational modifications the host cannot perform, or because purification is expensive. These claims are rarely quantified, and they are almost never compared against each other on a common scale.

This work asks a narrower and answerable question: **for a panel of nine food proteins, what does each candidate bottleneck actually cost, and which of them is binding?** The analysis is entirely computational and entirely reproducible from the accompanying repository.

Three things are established. First, the stoichiometric ceiling is nearly flat across the panel. Maximum theoretical yields computed by installing explicit, sequence-derived synthesis and secretion reactions into the genome-scale metabolic model iMT1026v3 of *K. phaffii* span only about 9% between the cheapest and the most expensive of the four caseins, β-lactoglobulin, α-lactalbumin, ovalbumin, soy leghemoglobin C2 and bovine lactoferrin, while the choice of carbon source moves the ceiling by roughly 30%. Within the post-translational load, phosphorylation is energetically free — charging α-S2-casein's thirteen phosphoserines costs under 0.12% of yield — whereas N-glycosylation is expensive and becomes dominant under the hypermannosylation that *K. phaffii* performs natively.

Second, realised production is nowhere near that ceiling. Joining the model-derived ceilings to a purpose-built database of **306 published titres**, each carrying a verbatim evidential quotation, a resolved identifier and a confidence grade, gives 75 high-confidence records of secreted production over 26 proteins whose sequences were verified by checksum against UniProtKB. Expressing each titre as a fraction of *its own* protein's ceiling: the ceiling has a log₁₀ standard deviation of **0.029** while utilisation of it has a log₁₀ standard deviation of **0.851**, with a median of 0.40% and an interquartile range of 0.07% to 2.1%. **The outcome is about thirty times more dispersed than the bound.** The best process in the corpus reached 8.0% of its ceiling and the median reached a four-hundredth of it. Metabolic cost is therefore excluded as the explanation of why some food proteins are harder to make than others, and the exclusion rests on the shape of a distribution rather than on a ratio between two records.

Third, and the answer to the question the thesis asks: **about half the dispersion in realised production is reproduced within single proteins.** Joining every qualifying record to its ceiling on the UniProt accession rather than on a protein name lets one protein appear as many times as it has been published, and once it can, the mean log₁₀ spread *inside* a single protein is 0.638 against 0.892 across the whole 88-record corpus. Hen ovalbumin spans 200-fold across four records; κ-casein spans 102-fold across five; human α-lactalbumin 115-fold across six. A crossed random-effects model over 88 records, 30 proteins and 52 studies places 44% of the variance between proteins, 16% between studies of the same protein and 40% within studies, and adding the seven sequence features halves the between-protein component without touching the residual — the signature of regressors absorbing protein identity rather than predicting from it. Cultivation mode is the one large and unambiguous effect: a shake flask costs a factor of five against a batch bioreactor (*p* = 0.002). Since half the dispersion occurs where the sequence does not vary at all, **no sequence-derived quantity can account for it**, and this is reported as an association and a negative result about method rather than as an identification of any mechanism.

Fourth, a direct test of what does explain it, which returns a null consistent with the above. Across 53 high-confidence records of secreted production in *K. phaffii* covering 25 distinct proteins, with seven mechanistic sequence features specified in advance, **no feature is associated with titre**: maximum theoretical yield at ρ = +0.06, disulfide density at ρ = −0.19, predicted disorder at ρ = −0.01, nothing reaching an uncorrected p of 0.05 let alone a corrected one. An earlier version of this work reported disulfide density as a significant association; re-reading disulfide counts from the UniProt feature tables instead of assuming that cysteines pair — an assumption wrong for 15 of 36 proteins — removed it, and the thesis reports that decomposition rather than the finding. Cross-validated against two nested controls, a single indicator of whether the run was in a bioreactor recovers 15.9% of the prediction error against a predict-the-mean baseline, adding the seven sequence features *reduces* that to 10.1%, and the sequence features alone are 8.3% worse than predicting the mean. The signal in the published record is overwhelmingly in the experiment rather than in the protein: **published titres are largely a record of how much engineering each protein has received rather than of how hard each protein is.**

Two candidate positive findings arose during this work and neither survived audit, which is reported rather than concealed. The more instructive of the two is documented in Appendix F: a correlation between mean predicted disorder and ceiling utilisation, at ρ = −0.833 with a permutation p of 0.00025, FDR control across 32 features and a leave-one-out check that held throughout, fell to ρ = −0.483, p = 0.117 once three faults in the underlying titre records were corrected. **Four statistical controls that are normally treated as sufficient all passed on data that did not mean what its column headings said.** The direction of the effect is consistent across every fit, so the thesis carries it as an untested hypothesis and names the measurement that would decide it: titres for α-S1-, α-S2- and β-casein, the three most disordered proteins in the target class and the three with no published number in any microbial host.

The practical consequences are developed in two further chapters: protein-specific metabolic engineering targets obtained by flux scanning with enforced objective function, and a techno-economic model in which every parameter carries a source and the result is reported against the stoichiometric cost floor implied by the computed yields. The cost analysis finds a floor near $1/kg of raw carbon against a modelled plant cost near $43/kg, and identifies recovery, titre and plant utilisation — not substrate price — as what determines whether precision-fermented proteins reach commodity economics.

The contribution of this thesis is not a new protein, a new strain or a new tool, and it is deliberately narrower than it first appears. Computing per-protein synthesis costs in a genome-scale model is not new: Feizi and co-workers did it across the yeast proteome in 2013, Gutierrez and co-workers reconstructed the mammalian secretory pathway and its per-protein costs in 2020, and pcSecYeast built protein-specific secretion reactions with per-protein bottleneck calls in 2022. Predicting producibility from sequence is not new either. Those steps are used here, with citation, as tools rather than claimed as findings.

What is new is the **decomposition**: computing each food protein's own stoichiometric ceiling, expressing every published titre as a fraction of *its own* ceiling rather than comparing titres to each other, and attributing the residual. That measurement has not been made for these proteins in these hosts, and it is what licenses the central claim — that the binding constraint on animal-free food proteins is not what it costs the cell to build them.

The decomposition's sharpest single application is to the commercially most important target. Intact bovine κ-casein, whose first published titres in *K. phaffii* appeared during this work at 28–133 mg L⁻¹, has one of the **highest** stoichiometric ceilings in the corpus and the lowest utilisation of it: it is metabolically cheap to build, carrying no glycans, no appreciable disulfide load and phosphorylation that costs nothing measurable, and it reaches 0.06% of what its host's stoichiometry permits. The casein problem is not a cost problem.

# Chapter 1 — Introduction

## 1.1 What precision fermentation is, and what it is not

Fermentation has been used to transform food for several thousand years. What distinguishes *precision* fermentation from that tradition is not the vessel, the organism or the sugar: it is the specificity of the instruction. In traditional fermentation a microbial consortium is given a substrate and allowed to do what its metabolism does — to acidify milk, to leaven dough, to produce ethanol. The product is the aggregate output of an organism behaving as itself. In precision fermentation the organism is given a gene it does not possess, and its protein synthesis machinery is redirected to manufacture one defined molecule, usually a single protein, which is then separated from the cells and sold as an ingredient.

The distinction matters because it determines what can go wrong. A traditional fermentation fails when the culture is contaminated or the conditions drift. A precision fermentation can fail in a much larger number of ways, because the host is being asked to do something it was never selected to do: to transcribe a foreign gene at high rate, to translate a messenger RNA whose codon composition belongs to another kingdom, to fold a polypeptide whose native folding environment was a mammary gland or a plant root nodule, to install chemical modifications it may not possess the enzymes for, to push the finished molecule out through a secretory pathway evolved for its own proteins, and to do all of this while still growing.

The technology is no longer speculative. Recombinant chymosin, produced in *Aspergillus niger* and *Kluyveromyces lactis*, has been used in the majority of industrial cheesemaking for decades and is the proof that the business model works. More recently, bovine β-lactoglobulin produced in the filamentous fungus *Trichoderma reesei* received a "no questions" letter from the United States Food and Drug Administration under GRAS Notice 863 in March 2020, and soy leghemoglobin produced in the methylotrophic yeast *Komagataella phaffii* — the protein responsible for the meat-like flavour and appearance of a widely sold plant-based burger — was cleared under GRAS Notice 737. Egg-white protein from *K. phaffii* followed under GRAS Notice 1104 in October 2023. These are the two hosts and several of the proteins that this thesis analyses, which means the work rests on an established and documented industrial reality rather than on a hypothetical one.

## 1.2 Why the proteins in this panel

Five product categories drive the field, and they map onto a panel of nine proteins analysed here.

**Caseins** (α-S1, α-S2, β and κ) make up roughly 80% of the protein in bovine milk and are what makes cheese behave like cheese. They are intrinsically disordered phosphoproteins that assemble into colloidal micelles stabilised by calcium phosphate nanoclusters nucleated on clusters of phosphorylated serine residues. They are the hardest target in the panel and also the most commercially valuable, because no whey protein can replace them in a melting, stretching cheese.

**Whey proteins** — β-lactoglobulin and α-lactalbumin — are small, globular, disulfide-bonded and far better behaved. β-lactoglobulin is the single most successful precision-fermented dairy protein to date; α-lactalbumin is the principal protein of human milk and a target for infant formula.

**Lactoferrin** is an iron-binding, antimicrobial milk protein with sixteen disulfide bonds and five N-glycosylation sites, and it is included here precisely because it carries the heaviest folding and modification load of anything in the panel.

**Ovalbumin** is the dominant egg-white protein, the target for animal-free egg products, and an unusual case because it is secreted without cleavage of an N-terminal signal peptide.

**Soy leghemoglobin C2** is a plant haemoglobin and the only cofactor-dependent protein in the panel; producing it requires the host to supply protohaem at a rate it does not normally need to.

Taken together the panel spans a wide range of the properties a host must cope with: intrinsic disorder against tight globular folds, zero to sixteen disulfide bonds, zero to five glycosylation sites, zero to thirteen phosphorylation sites, a cofactor requirement, and a nearly sixfold range of chain length. That spread is what makes a comparative analysis informative.

## 1.3 The problem this thesis addresses

The literature of this field names its central challenge consistently: achieving correct folding, achieving the right post-translational modifications, and scaling downstream purification affordably. The project brief that initiated this work names the same three. What the literature does not do is quantify them against one another.

This is not a trivial omission. "Caseins are hard because they require phosphorylation" is a causal claim with a measurable content: it says that the phosphorylation requirement imposes a cost that the host cannot bear. If that cost turns out to be negligible, the sentence is still true in some sense — yeast genuinely cannot phosphorylate casein the way a mammary cell does — but the *reason* it matters is completely different. It is not that phosphorylation is expensive; it is that the enzyme is absent and the product is therefore wrong, not scarce. Those two readings imply different engineering programmes: the first says improve the energetics, the second says transplant a kinase. Deciding between them requires a number.

The same ambiguity runs through every other claim in the field. Is N-glycosylation a problem because it burdens metabolism, or because yeast glycans are the wrong glycans? Is a long, disulfide-rich protein hard because it is expensive to build, or because the oxidative folding machinery of the endoplasmic reticulum saturates? Is leghemoglobin limited by the energy of haem synthesis or by the flux capacity of a pathway that normally supplies only the cell's own cytochromes? In every case, two mechanisms with very different remedies are being described by the same word, *bottleneck*, and the field's reviews move between them without distinguishing.

## 1.4 The question, and how it is made answerable

The question this thesis asks is:

> **Why do recombinant proteins with remarkably similar stoichiometric production ceilings exhibit such dramatically different levels of realised production?**

The question is put that way because the premise is itself a result, and it is the first thing the thesis establishes. Across 26 proteins the stoichiometric ceiling has a log₁₀ standard deviation of 0.029 — a 1.31-fold range from the cheapest to the most expensive — while utilisation of that ceiling has a log₁₀ standard deviation of 0.839 and an interquartile range from 0.07% to 2.1%. The outcome is roughly thirty times more dispersed than the bound. Once that is measured, "why are some food proteins harder to make?" stops being a question about cost, because cost has been shown not to vary enough to be the answer.

The answer the thesis reaches is that the dispersion is mostly not a property of the protein. Joining every qualifying published record to its own protein's ceiling — so that one protein appears as many times as it has been published — shows that the mean spread *within* a single protein is 0.638 in log₁₀ against 0.892 across the whole corpus. Hen ovalbumin spans 200-fold across four records; κ-casein spans 102-fold across five. Roughly half the variation in realised production is reproduced where the sequence does not vary at all, and a variance decomposition with crossed random effects for protein and study puts 44% of the variance between proteins, 16% between studies of the same protein and 40% within studies. Cultivation mode is the one large, unambiguous fixed effect: a shake flask costs a factor of five against a batch bioreactor. This is an association and a negative result about method, not an identification of a mechanism, and §5.2.2 and §6.6 are careful about the difference.

This is made answerable by a decomposition in three steps.

The first step establishes the **ceiling**. For each protein, its exact amino-acid composition, disulfide count, glycosylation sites, phosphosites and cofactor requirement are converted into an explicit synthesis and secretion reaction and installed into the genome-scale metabolic model iMT1026v3 of *K. phaffii*. Maximising that reaction under a fixed substrate supply gives the maximum theoretical yield in grams of protein per gram of glycerol, methanol or glucose: a hard upper bound that no strain, however engineered, can exceed while obeying the stoichiometry of its own metabolism.

The second step establishes the **floor**: what has actually been achieved. This required building a database of published titres from primary literature, because no suitable compilation exists. Every record carries a verbatim quotation of the sentence reporting the number, a citation, and a confidence grade, so that the comparison rests on evidence that can be checked rather than on recollection.

The third step is the **decomposition**. Expressing each published titre as a fraction of that protein's own ceiling converts incomparable numbers into a comparable one, and the residual — the part of the gap that the ceiling does not explain — becomes an object that can itself be analysed against the properties of the protein.

## 1.5 What is new here, stated conservatively

Honesty about novelty is more useful than enthusiasm about it, and the prior-art audit conducted for this thesis (Chapter 2) found a great deal of relevant prior work.

Computing per-protein biosynthetic costs in a metabolic model is established practice. Feizi and colleagues computed ATP, GTP and glycosylation-donor costs across the yeast proteome in 2013. Gutierrez and colleagues reconstructed the mammalian secretory pathway in 2020 and computed per-protein secretion costs and limits. The proteome-constrained model pcSecYeast built protein-specific secretion reactions from sequence and post-translational requirements for eight recombinant proteins, including a haemoglobin, and called the bottleneck for each. Predicting expression or secretion success from sequence features is likewise a developed field, from Presep for *P. pastoris* in 2013 through secretome-scale analyses in 2025, and the phrase "predicting protein producibility" was already used for filamentous fungi in 2017. A publicly visible but unpublished repository is, concurrently with this work, building translation, glycosylation and disulfide cost reactions on the same iMT1026v3 model.

None of those steps is claimed here as a discovery. They are used as instruments, with citation, in Chapters 3 and 4.

What has not been done, and what this thesis contributes, is the decomposition itself, together with the evidence base that makes it checkable:

1. **A curated, quotation-backed corpus of 306 published secretion titres**, which is a contribution in its own right rather than supporting material. Every record carries the verbatim sentence from its source that states the number, a DOI resolved against Crossref and confirmed at doi.org, the host and strain, the cultivation mode, a confidence grade, and a classification as food protein, food or industrial enzyme, research reagent or human therapeutic. No compilation of this kind existed for these hosts. Its design is load-bearing rather than decorative: §5.3 and Appendix F each document a conclusion that would have been wrong without it, and in one case it was the quoted sentence — the words "cell lysis, and purification" — that exposed an intracellular yield recorded as a secretion titre.
2. **Per-protein stoichiometric ceilings for named food proteins** in *K. phaffii*, resolved by carbon source and by post-translational component, including the separation of the glycoprotein mass yield from the polypeptide yield that a food application actually cares about.
3. **Each published titre expressed as a fraction of its own protein's ceiling**, which converts the field's incommensurable numbers into one comparable quantity, and the attribution of the residual — including the negative results, which turn out to be the informative ones, and two candidate positive findings that were withdrawn under audit rather than published.

The caseins are the central test case throughout rather than an illustration. They are the commercially most important class in the panel, the class the field names as hardest, the class whose difficulty is conventionally attributed to disorder, and — until this work — the class with almost no published quantitative titre. They are also the class where the decomposition speaks most clearly, because they are metabolically among the *cheapest* proteins to build and among the worst produced.

## 1.6 Structure of the thesis

Chapter 2 reviews the field and reports the prior-art audit in full, including the work that comes closest to this one and exactly how close it comes. Chapter 3 sets out the computational methods, the data provenance rules and the verification procedures, including the independent reimplementation that cross-checked the central numbers. Chapter 4 computes the stoichiometric ceilings and decomposes the post-translational load. Chapter 5 presents the ceiling–gap measurement. Chapter 6 tests what the gap tracks and reports both the positive and the negative findings. Chapter 7 derives protein-specific metabolic engineering targets. Chapter 8 develops the techno-economic consequences. Chapter 9 discusses limitations, the confounds that the analysis cannot remove, and the minimal wet-laboratory programme that would test the predictions made here.


# Chapter 2 — Literature review

Precision fermentation of food proteins sits at the junction of four literatures that do not usually speak to one another: the industrial and regulatory record of a young commercial sector; the deep host-engineering literature of two microorganisms, one yeast and one filamentous fungus; the structural and dairy-science literature of the proteins themselves; and the computational literature of metabolic modelling and sequence-based prediction. This chapter reads across all four, because the question this thesis asks — why proteins with nearly identical stoichiometric production ceilings reach titres differing by orders of magnitude — cannot be posed, let alone answered, from inside any one of them.

The chapter has three jobs. The first is the ordinary one of a literature review: to establish what is known, with sources. The second is to extract from each literature the specific quantities this thesis later uses or tests, and to say where those quantities came from. The third, set out in §2.8, is to establish by systematic search which parts of the proposed contribution have already been made by someone else, because a novelty claim that has not been audited is not a claim but a hope.

**The corpus this chapter reads.** The literature corpus assembled for this thesis contains 230 references across nine topic clusters. Every identifier was resolved against Crossref and confirmed independently at doi.org: 189 of the 230 carry a verified DOI, the remaining 41 have none and the reason is stated for each, and none was inferred. That pass was treated as blocking rather than cosmetic, because an earlier audit found that inferred identifiers were wrong roughly half the time — and an inferred DOI looks exactly like a resolved one and fails silently. Resolution corrected 11 first authors and 12 years that would otherwise have become wrong in-text citations, recovered 48 identifiers that an initial automated pass had missed, and exposed two papers the bibliography had listed twice. Counts by cluster: industry and regulation 21; *Komagataella phaffii* as a host 34; *Trichoderma reesei* as a host 17; milk proteins 31; egg and haem proteins 16; computational methods 49; genome-scale modelling and protein cost 28; techno-economics and life-cycle assessment 17; prior art on producibility prediction 17.

## 2.1 The industrial and regulatory state of the field

### 2.1.1 What has actually been cleared

Precision fermentation of food proteins passed from demonstration to commerce between 2018 and 2025, and the regulatory record is the cleanest available evidence of where the technology genuinely works, because a regulatory filing requires a real process at a real scale with a characterised product. Three clearances anchor the field.

**Soy leghemoglobin in *Pichia pastoris*** was the first of the modern wave. The United States Food and Drug Administration issued its response to GRAS Notice 737 in 2018, and in 2019 the Federal Register separately listed soy leghemoglobin as a colour additive exempt from certification (84 FR 37573) — a second and independent approval, reflecting that the protein's commercial function is as much visual as nutritional. The safety dossier was supported by a published evaluation of allergenic and toxicological risk of the *P. pastoris*-expressed protein (Jin et al. 2018, 10.1002/mnfr.201700297).

**Bovine β-lactoglobulin in *Trichoderma reesei*** followed under GRAS Notice 863, with the agency's "no questions" response in March 2020. This is the single most important precedent in the field for dairy proteins, both because it established that a filamentous fungus can be a food-protein host and because the resulting product reached retail at scale.

**Egg-white protein in *Komagataella phaffii*** (strain ATCC GSD-1235) was cleared under GRAS Notice 1104, closed 17 October 2023, extending the same regulatory pathway from dairy to egg.

A fourth precedent predates all of these and is worth stating because it is the proof that the business model works at commodity scale: recombinant chymosin, produced in *Aspergillus niger* var. *awamori* and in *Kluyveromyces lactis*, has been used in the majority of industrial cheesemaking since the 1990s. Chymosin is an enzyme rather than a structural food protein, so the technical problem is different — a few milligrams per litre of a catalyst can replace an animal product, where a structural protein must be produced in kilograms — but the regulatory and consumer-acceptance path was cleared by it. The recombinant production of bovine chymosin B in *P. pastoris* under the AOX1 promoter, optimised in a bioreactor, is documented by Noseda and colleagues (2013, 10.1016/j.pep.2013.08.018).

### 2.1.2 Where the record thins

Beyond those clearances the public record thins into self-affirmation and trade press, and this thesis treats it accordingly. Animal-free casein has been self-determined as GRAS by at least one company (2024); lactoferrin has been claimed as self-affirmed GRAS and subsequently reported as receiving FDA clearance (2024–2025); an ovalbumin producer submitted a formal GRAS notification in December 2024 without a notice number traceable in the sources retrieved here; and an animal-free whey producer was reported in 2025 to have received a no-questions letter. Trade analyses place the running total of FDA-cleared precision-fermented proteins at roughly a dozen, with four arriving in 2025 alone, and note that the European Union has approved none under its novel-foods framework while the United States has cleared several — a regulatory asymmetry that shapes where companies choose to launch.

All of these are recorded in the bibliography as industry-type references and **none is used to support a quantitative claim in this thesis**. The distinction matters for a specific reason that §3.8 develops: a company press release reporting "commercial-scale production" carries no titre, no cultivation mode and no basis, and a corpus that admitted such statements as data would be measuring announcements rather than fermentations.

A separate and substantial regulatory-science literature is emerging around the safety assessment of these products, and it is worth noting even though this thesis makes no safety claims. A scientific expert panel has published a study roadmap for evaluating recombinant human lactoferrin from *K. phaffii* as a food ingredient (Malinczak et al. 2024, 10.1016/j.fct.2024.114817), and the food-allergy risk of the same protein has been separately evaluated (Anaya et al. 2024, 10.3389/fimmu.2024.1380028). The existence of this literature is evidence that lactoferrin is being taken seriously as a near-term commercial target, which is one reason it is in this thesis's panel.

### 2.1.3 The financial context, and why it determines what counts as a useful result

One sentence of financial context changes what a thesis in this area should try to do. Fermentation-sector investment fell from $632 million in 2024 to $357 million in 2025 according to the Good Food Institute's industry analysis — a contraction of more than 40% in a single year. In an expanding funding environment, work that adds another candidate engineering target has value because someone will try all of them. In a contracting one, work that tells a developer *which* bottleneck is worth spending money on is worth considerably more than work that adds a candidate, because the developer can now afford to test one hypothesis rather than five.

This is the practical justification for the decomposition this thesis performs. Identifying a bottleneck is useful; excluding one with a number is more useful, because exclusion is what allows a programme to be narrowed.

## 2.2 *Komagataella phaffii* as a cell factory

*K. phaffii*, the methylotrophic yeast long and still widely known as *Pichia pastoris*, is the dominant host for secreted recombinant protein production in food biotechnology, and the host this thesis models. Its advantages are documented across three decades and are worth stating precisely, because each of them is a reason that the stoichiometric ceiling computed in Chapter 4 is a meaningful quantity rather than an abstraction.

It grows to very high cell density — dry cell weights of 100 g L⁻¹ and above — on simple defined media containing no complex nitrogen source, which is what makes the substrate-loading figure that Chapter 5 divides by a well-defined number. It carries a strong, tightly regulated, methanol-inducible promoter (AOX1) alongside strong constitutive alternatives (GAP), so that growth and production can be separated in time. It secretes heterologous protein efficiently against a very low background of native secreted protein, which is what makes a downstream process affordable. And it does not install the hyper-immunogenic terminal α-1,3-mannose linkage found in *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, which is what made it acceptable as a host for products intended for human consumption in the first place.

Five strands of this host's literature bear directly on the present work.

### 2.2.1 Promoters and the methanol problem

The AOX1 promoter remains the workhorse of the platform and is the reference case in essentially every comparative study. It is also a liability at scale: methanol is flammable, toxic, generates a large heat load on oxidation, and requires feed control tight enough that loss of control is a batch-destroying event. That liability has driven a sustained and still-active engineering literature on methanol-free induction.

Four approaches are represented in the corpus. **Retrofitting** existing AOX1-based strains for methanol-independent expression keeps the promoter and changes its regulation (Schleichert et al. 2026, 10.1007/978-1-0716-4779-0_19). **Trans-acting element engineering** decouples AOX1 activity from methanol directly and has been demonstrated with a glucose–glycerol-shift induction scheme (Wang et al. 2017, 10.1038/srep41850), with the transcriptional regulatory networks underlying that decoupling subsequently mapped (Shi et al. 2020, 10.1186/s40643-020-00306-w). **Alternative promoters** have been benchmarked against the standard set, including ADH3 (Karaoglan et al. 2016, 10.1016/j.pep.2016.01.017). And **auto-induction** schemes have been developed for particularly difficult product classes such as membrane proteins (Lee et al. 2017, 10.1016/j.pep.2017.06.006).

This literature matters to Chapter 4 in a way that is easy to miss. The stoichiometric yield of protein on methanol is substantially worse than on glycerol, because methanol assimilation through the xylulose-monophosphate cycle costs ATP and reducing equivalents that glycerol assimilation does not. The methanol penalty computed in Chapter 4 is therefore not an abstract modelling result: it is a quantification of one of the costs that this entire body of promoter-engineering work is trying to avoid, and it gives a number to the question of how much a methanol-free process is worth in yield terms alone, before safety and capital are considered.

### 2.2.2 Secretion leaders

The *S. cerevisiae* α-mating-factor prepro leader is the default secretion signal for this host and is not always the best one. The comparison of the α-factor leader against native and endogenous signal peptides is a recurring theme, examined systematically by Zou and colleagues (2022, 10.1186/s13068-022-02243-6), and the answer is protein-dependent rather than general.

Two specific findings from this strand are load-bearing later. First, *K. phaffii* YPS1-5 encodes a Bar1-like protease that degrades α-factor (Heistinger et al. 2020, 10.1093/femsyr/foaa024) — which means the default leader is itself a substrate for a host protease, and that leader choice and proteolysis are not independent variables. Second, native secretion signals combined with a higher induction temperature have been shown to outperform the default for a difficult plant prolamin (Büchner et al. 2025, 10.1186/s12934-025-02809-7), which is a reminder that leader performance is conditional on process conditions rather than intrinsic.

For the present analysis the methodological consequence is stated in Chapter 3: every construct designed in this thesis uses a single common secretion leader, which removes leader choice as a variable rather than attempting to predict it. The cost of that decision is that the ceiling computed here is a ceiling for a particular secretion architecture, and the literature above establishes that the architecture matters.

### 2.2.3 Folding, the unfolded protein response, and secretory capacity

Overexpression of a secreted protein imposes measurable stress on the endoplasmic reticulum, and the host's response to that stress is the single best-characterised candidate mechanism for the gap this thesis measures.

The mechanism is understood in outline. Activation of the unfolded protein response in *P. pastoris* requires splicing of a *HAC1* mRNA intron and retention of the C-terminal tail of Hac1p (Whyteside et al. 2011, 10.1016/j.febslet.2011.02.036). The UPR is active in this organism without external stressing stimuli (Fauzee et al. 2020, 10.1093/femsyr/foaa053), and Ire1 participates in cytosolic and nuclear protein quality control independently of *HAC1* (Fauzee et al. 2023, 10.3389/fmicb.2023.1157146). The response is not specific to protein overload: it is also involved in osmotolerance (Dragosits et al. 2010, 10.1186/1471-2164-11-207), which means that UPR activation in a production process is not by itself evidence of a folding problem.

The engineering literature built on this mechanism is extensive and its results are instructive. Regulating the UPR activator Hac1p improved production of a thermostable α-amylase (Huang et al. 2017, 10.1007/s00449-016-1701-y); co-expression of *HAC1* improved stem bromelain production (Varadharaj et al. 2025, 10.1016/j.pep.2025.106804); engineering translocation together with the UPR raised secretion of a human hyaluronidase (Zhang et al. 2024, 10.1007/s00253-023-12878-6); and enhancing the secretion pathway alongside energy metabolism improved activity of a recombinant product (Shi et al. 2026, 10.1016/j.bej.2025.109935). A systematic deletion of low-essentiality secretion-associated genes has recently been shown to enhance production (Ford et al. 2026, 10.1186/s12934-026-03009-7), which is a different and more surgical approach to the same target. Two reviews synthesise the strategy space: Raschmanová and colleagues on UPR pathway engineering specifically (2021, 10.1007/s00253-021-11336-5) and Puxbaum and colleagues on the broader folding-and-secretion challenge, under the title "Quo vadis?" (2015, 10.1007/s00253-015-6470-z).

This is the mechanism that remains the leading candidate for the residual gap of Chapter 5, and the candidate is strong because the host literature responds to it: interventions aimed at folding and secretion capacity raise titres repeatedly and across unrelated products. The positive evidence for it in this thesis is that mechanism as reported in the primary sources, not anything measured here; §6.2.1 records why.

### 2.2.4 Proteolysis

Proteolytic degradation of secreted product is the other recurring failure mode, and it is central to the casein result of §5.3. *K. phaffii* secretes and retains proteases that act on heterologous product, the vacuolar aspartyl protease Pep4 and the yapsin Yps1 among them, and deletion of these genes is a standard first intervention. The Bar1-like activity of YPS1-5 noted above is a specific instance with a specific consequence for the default secretion leader.

What makes this strand decisive for the caseins is that the intervention is documented *not* to be sufficient. The 2026 κ-casein study discussed in §5.3 reports that *pep4* and *yps1* knockouts did not fully stop degradation of the product. For an intrinsically disordered protein with no compact fold to protect its backbone, the set of proteases that must be removed may be considerably larger than the set that suffices for a globular product, and the available genetic interventions do not obviously scale to it.

### 2.2.5 Glycosylation

*K. phaffii* transfers a core Man₈GlcNAc₂ glycan in the endoplasmic reticulum and then extends it in the Golgi, producing high-mannose structures that are not the structures a mammalian food protein would carry natively. This has consequences on three axes, and they are usually conflated.

**Product identity.** A hypermannosylated lactoferrin is not bovine lactoferrin. For a food application this may be acceptable, irrelevant or disqualifying depending on whether the glycan affects function, digestion or allergenicity, and the question is empirical in each case.

**Metabolic cost.** Outer-chain extension consumes GDP-mannose, and the consumption scales with the number of mannose residues added. This is the one post-translational modification in the panel that is genuinely expensive, and Chapter 4 quantifies it: under hypermannosylation the penalty reaches up to 41% of polypeptide yield. The direct methodological ancestor of that calculation is the genome-scale metabolic model of *P. pastoris* with native and humanised glycosylation built by Irani and colleagues (2016, 10.1002/bit.25863), and the glycosylation accounting in Chapter 4 is an application of their approach to a different panel rather than a new method.

**Engineering tractability.** The glyco-engineering literature in this host is mature. The glycosylation pathway was modified as early as 1998 (Martinet et al., 10.1023/a:1005340806821); an artificial glycosylation pathway blocked in core oligosaccharide assembly was built to produce complex humanised glycoproteins with terminal galactose (Bobrowicz et al. 2004, 10.1093/glycob/cwh104); humanised IgGs were optimised in glyco-engineered *P. pastoris* (Li et al. 2006, 10.1038/nbt1178); recombinant human lactoferrin was expressed in a glyco-engineered strain with terminal sialic acid (Choi et al. 2008, 10.1007/s10719-008-9123-y); and homogeneous N-GlcNAc protein has been produced in this host (Wang et al. 2020, 10.1186/s12934-020-1280-0). The adaptive landscape of glycosylation-deficient populations has itself been mapped (Moser et al. 2017, 10.1186/s12864-017-3952-7), which matters because glyco-engineered strains carry fitness costs that a production process must absorb.

The practical conclusion for Chapter 7 is that glycosylation is the one post-translational problem in this panel for which the cost is real, the quantity is computable, and the engineering route is already demonstrated — which is a rare combination in this field and is why it is the one modification whose cost Chapter 4 reports as actionable rather than merely descriptive.

### 2.2.6 Host physiology under production load

A smaller but important strand measures what production does to the cell. Quantitative metabolomics of amino acid metabolism in recombinant *P. pastoris* under different oxygen availabilities (Carnicer et al. 2012, 10.1186/1475-2859-11-83) and metabolic flux profiling of a secreting strain on glucose–methanol mixtures (Jordà et al. 2012, 10.1186/1475-2859-11-57) established the fluxomic baseline. Contextualised genome-scale modelling revealed high-order metabolic effects of growth rate and oxygenation (Torres et al. 2019, 10.1016/j.mec.2019.e00103). Production dynamics and physiological adaptation have been characterised at near-zero growth rates (Rebnegger et al. 2024, 10.1186/s12934-024-02314-3) and metabolic rewiring at extremely slow growth separately described (Coltman et al. 2023). Amino acid metabolism has been customised for recombinant production (Rußmayer et al. 2023, 10.1002/biot.202300033), and the metabolic impact of redox perturbation under methanolic growth has recently been assessed (Antón et al. 2026, 10.1186/s12934-026-03147-y).

Two findings from this strand are directly relevant to the interpretation of Chapter 5. First, a macrokinetic model-based comparative meta-analysis of recombinant production under the AOX1 promoter (Barrigón et al. 2015, 10.1002/bit.25518) established that specific productivity is a function of growth rate in a way that differs between products — which is a within-host, within-promoter source of titre variation entirely independent of the protein's composition, and therefore part of the "process" variance that §5.2.2 finds dominates. Second, the oxygen transfer rate has been used as an indicator of metabolic burden to predict high-producing strains (Wollborn et al. 2022, 10.1038/s41598-022-15086-w), which is an empirical counterpart to the oxygen-bound calculation of §5.2.4.

Two recent reviews cover the platform as a whole and are useful entry points: Lv and colleagues on opportunities and challenges in recombinant expression in *K. phaffii* (2025, 10.1093/femsyr/foaf010) and Huang and colleagues on systematic optimisation including AI-driven approaches (2026, 10.1016/j.engmic.2026.100305). Strategy-level syntheses of cell engineering (Bustos et al. 2022, 10.3390/metabo12040346), metabolic engineering (Peña et al. 2018, 10.1016/j.ymben.2018.04.017), yeast secretion and display platform design (Xu et al. 2025, 10.1080/07388551.2024.2385996) and metabolic engineering for both proteins and small molecules (Xin et al. 2026, 10.1128/aem.00110-26) complete the picture.

## 2.3 *Trichoderma reesei* as a production host

*T. reesei* is the other industrially proven host for this product class, and the one that produced the first regulatory-cleared precision-fermented dairy protein. It is included in this thesis because excluding it would make the titre corpus a study of one organism, and because its failure modes are different enough from the yeast's to be informative.

### 2.3.1 Secretory capacity, and the carrier trick

The organism's distinguishing property is secretory capacity. *T. reesei* was selected industrially for decades for cellulase titres measured in tens of grams per litre, and that machinery can in principle be redirected to a foreign protein. The classic enabling device is fusion to a carrier, most often cellobiohydrolase I: Nyyssönen and colleagues (1995, 10.1007/bf00311884) established the multiple roles of CBHI in enhancing production of fusion antibodies, and the approach remains standard. Hydrophobin fusions serve a related purpose for intracellular production and recovery (Mustalahti et al. 2013, 10.1016/j.nbt.2011.09.006). Heterologous expression using the *cbhII* promoter (Meng et al. 2013, 10.1016/j.plasmid.2013.05.003) and transformation with a heterologous glucoamylase as early as 1993 (Joutsjoki et al., 10.1007/bf00351796) mark the span of the approach.

The kinetics of this machinery have been studied directly, with CBHI itself as the model glycoprotein (Pakula et al. 2000, 10.1099/00221287-146-1-223), and protein disulfide isomerase homologues have been shown to contribute to cellobiohydrolase production (Wang et al. 2015, 10.1016/j.enzmictec.2015.05.004) — the fungal counterpart of the folding-capacity literature in the yeast.

The carrier trick has a methodological consequence this thesis had to act on. A reported titre for a carrier fusion includes the carrier's mass, and the carrier is chosen precisely because it improves secretion. Such a record is not a measurement of the protein of interest. §5.4 and Appendix F record a case where exactly this kind of record entered the corpus and had to be removed, and the corpus filter now excludes fusions, carriers and chimaeras by pattern.

### 2.3.2 Proteolysis and production load

Two practical problems recur. The first is proteolysis: the organism secretes proteases that degrade heterologous product, and protease-deletion strains are the standard remedy. Disruption of the alkaline serine protease combined with a pH control strategy enhances heterologous production (Zhang et al. 2014, 10.1016/j.plasmid.2014.01.001), and deletion of the p53-like regulator Vib1 produced a novel expression platform (Sun et al. 2022, 10.1016/j.enzmictec.2022.109993). Genetic tools enabling highly pure protein production — that is, production against a suppressed cellulase background — were developed by Rantasalo and colleagues (2019, 10.1038/s41598-019-41573-8), and are what make the host usable for a food protein that must be purified affordably.

The second problem is the production load itself, which has been examined at genome scale (Pakula et al. 2016, 10.1186/s13068-016-0547-5). Both problems limit how far the host's cellulase-scale capacity transfers to a foreign protein, and the honest summary of the literature is that it transfers partially and unpredictably.

Recent reviews cover the host comprehensively: Li and colleagues on unlocking *T. reesei* as a super-host (2025, 10.1002/biot.70121), Liu and colleagues on heterologous production in filamentous fungi generally (2023, 10.1007/s00253-023-12660-8), and Hu and colleagues on engineering the organism into a programmable eukaryotic chassis for food raw materials (2026, 10.1016/j.tifs.2026.105871). The adjacent *Aspergillus* literature is relevant because it is where the food-enzyme precedent lives, and a mycotoxin-free *A. oryzae* strain lineage has been developed specifically for alternative-protein production at industrial scale (Lehmbeck et al. 2025, 10.1007/s00253-025-13482-6).

### 2.3.3 What the food-protein data in this host actually consist of

The food-protein literature in *T. reesei* is thin but decisive. The VTT work producing bovine β-lactoglobulin and hen ovalbumin by precision fermentation, with techno-functional testing of the products (Aro et al. 2023, 10.1016/j.foodres.2022.112131), is the primary source for the benchmark titres used in Chapter 5 and is one of the very few papers in this entire field that reports both a product mass and a functional characterisation.

The quantity of comparable fungal data is a limitation recorded in Chapter 9, and it is worth being precise about its nature. Of the 306 records in the corpus, 48 are from *T. reesei* against 140 from *K. phaffii*. More importantly, fungal papers report enzymatic activity or fold-change relative to a parent strain far more often than they report product mass in grams per litre. This is a property of the literature rather than an artefact of the search: an activity measurement is what a cellulase producer needs, and the field's reporting conventions were set by that application. For a structural food protein, where there is no activity to assay, the convention is a liability, and it is the single largest reason the corpus is smaller than the published literature would suggest.

## 2.4 The proteins

The nine proteins of the panel were chosen to span the properties a host must cope with, and each carries a distinct literature. This section establishes, for each, what the protein is, what modification it requires, what is known about producing it recombinantly, and — where the field has made a specific claim about its difficulty — what that claim is, so that Chapter 6 can test it rather than restate it.

### 2.4.1 Caseins and the phosphorylation problem

The caseins are not globular proteins and should not be analysed as if they were. They are intrinsically disordered phosphoproteins whose biological function is to carry calcium and phosphate at concentrations far above the solubility limit without precipitating, and whose food function — gelation, rennet coagulation, the melt and stretch of cheese — follows from that function rather than from any folded structure.

**The mechanism.** Clusters of phosphorylated serine residues sequester amorphous calcium phosphate as nanoclusters of a defined size, and the casein micelle is a sequestration structure built on that principle. The demonstration that a β-casein phosphopeptide can modulate calcium phosphate precipitation by forming amorphous dicalcium phosphate nanoclusters (Holt et al. 1996, 10.1042/bj3141035) is the founding experiment, extended into a core–shell model of the nanoclusters derived from sedimentation equilibrium and small-angle scattering (Holt et al. 1998, 10.1046/j.1432-1327.1998.2520073.x) and into chromatographic analysis of micelle substructure (Holt et al. 1998, 10.3168/jds.s0022-0302(98)75863-1). The model has been refined over three decades into explicit ion-equilibrium treatments of the micelle (Bijl et al. 2019, 10.1007/s00249-018-1330-2) and a quantitative nanocluster model of average size, size distribution and surface properties (Holt 2021, 10.1007/s00249-021-01533-5). Previously unclassified calcium phosphate structures within the micelle have since been identified experimentally (Hindmarsh et al. 2017, 10.3168/jds.2017-12623), the structural biology of phosphoprotein-sequestered nanoclusters has been reviewed (Lenton et al. 2020, 10.3390/cryst10090755), and the formation, stability and microstructure of nanoclusters sequestered by casein phosphopeptides has been characterised in a food-hydrocolloid context (Wang et al. 2024, 10.1016/j.foodhyd.2024.110315). Neutron reflectometry has resolved the co-adsorption of β-casein and calcium phosphate nanoclusters at interfaces (Follows et al. 2011, 10.1016/j.foodhyd.2010.09.001). Comparative work across twenty species establishes which features of casein and micelle structure are conserved and which are not (Holt et al. 2016, 10.1016/j.idairyj.2016.01.004), and general reviews cover structural and functional aspects (Bhat et al. 2016, 10.5772/64187) and the relation of micellar organisation to functional and nutritional properties (Runthala et al. 2023, 10.3390/molecules28052023).

**Why the modification is not decorative.** The result that matters most for precision fermentation is that dephosphorylated caseins lose the ability to form micelles. The kinase responsible in the mammary gland is of the Fam20C family, identified as a secreted kinase that phosphorylates extracellular proteins regulating biomineralisation (Tagliabracci et al. 2012, 10.1126/science.1217817). *K. phaffii* has no orthologue of it. A review dedicated precisely to this problem — the role of phosphorylation in heterologous casein functionality, and how to achieve it — appeared in 2025 (Mora Vásquez et al., 10.3390/biom15071031), which is the clearest sign that the field regards this as the central casein question.

**Where the field's language becomes ambiguous, and what Chapter 4 does to it.** The statement "caseins are hard because they need phosphorylation" admits two readings. It can mean that the phosphorylation is metabolically expensive, so that a host asked to install thirteen phosphates per chain pays a yield penalty. Or it can mean that the host lacks the kinase, so that the product is wrong rather than scarce. These readings imply different engineering programmes — improve the energetics, or transplant a kinase — and the field's reviews move between them without distinguishing. Chapter 4 shows that the first reading is quantitatively false: phosphorylation is essentially free in stoichiometric terms, at a cost of one ATP per site against a chain that costs thousands. The second reading is the whole of the problem.

**What has been produced.** A Wageningen thesis reports production of phosphorylated and functional α-S1-casein in *K. phaffii* (2023, edepot.wur.nl/700586), which is the most relevant single document in the literature for this thesis's central test case, and Balasubramanian's doctoral work on precision fermentation of milk proteins (2024, DTU) covers the problem space in depth. The structural analysis of breast-milk α-S1-casein showing that an α-helical conformation is required for TLR4 stimulation (Saenger et al. 2024, 10.3390/ijms25031743) is a reminder that "intrinsically disordered" does not mean structureless in every context, and that a recombinant casein's conformational state may matter for properties beyond cheese-making. Heterogeneity of human milk lactoproteins at the proteoform and peptidoform level has recently been mapped in a comparative mammalian context (Soceanu et al. 2026, 10.3390/biom16111607), which bears on what "the" casein sequence even means as a production target.

**Why they are the central test case of this thesis.** The caseins are the commercially most important class in the panel; the class the field names as hardest; the class whose difficulty is conventionally attributed to disorder and phosphorylation; and — until the corpus rebuild described in §5.3 — the class with almost no published quantitative titre. They are also the class where the decomposition speaks most clearly, because they are metabolically among the *cheapest* proteins in the panel to build and among the worst produced. A thesis arguing that cost does not explain the ordering could hardly ask for a better test case, and §5.3 treats it as one.

### 2.4.2 Whey proteins

β-lactoglobulin is a 162-residue lipocalin with a well-characterised eight-stranded antiparallel β-barrel, two disulfide bonds and one free cysteine. Its structure was solved in 1986 and its similarity to plasma retinol-binding protein noted at the time (Papiz et al., 10.1038/324383a0). It has been studied extensively as a ligand binder: interaction with retinol and fatty acids and the question of a biological function (Pérez et al. 1995, 10.3168/jds.s0022-0302(95)76713-3), palmitate binding within the central cavity (Wu et al. 1999, 10.1074/jbc.274.1.170), the evidence for and against a functional ligand-binding site (Kontopidis et al. 2002, 10.1016/s0022-2836(02)00017-7), and a review of binding properties, structure and function (Kontopidis et al. 2004, 10.3168/jds.s0022-0302(04)73222-1). For precision fermentation specifically, the physicochemical and emulsifying properties of engineered recombinant β-lactoglobulin variants have been characterised (Keppler et al. 2021, 10.1016/j.foodhyd.2020.106132) — one of the few studies that closes the loop from construct to food function.

α-lactalbumin is a 123-residue calcium-binding protein with four disulfide bonds, homologous to lysozyme, and functioning as the regulatory subunit of lactose synthase; its structure and function are reviewed by Permyakov and Berliner (2000, 10.1016/S0014-5793(00)01546-5). It is the principal protein of human milk and a target for infant formula, and efficient bioproduction of the human protein in *K. phaffii* has been reported (Deng et al. 2022, 10.1021/acs.jafc.1c07908). Production in a transplastomic plant system has recently been demonstrated as an alternative route (Ehsasatvatan et al. 2026, 10.1038/s41598-026-38965-y).

Both are small, globular, and free of essential post-translational modification, which is why the field's reviews place them in the tractable tier. **This thesis tests that placement rather than assuming it, and §5.2.5 reports that for α-lactalbumin it does not hold**: the human protein sits at 0.31% of its stoichiometric ceiling, below lactoferrin, ovalbumin and lysozyme. That is one of the few places where this analysis contradicts the published expert ordering, and it is reported as a contradiction rather than reconciled.

### 2.4.3 Lactoferrin

Bovine lactoferrin is a 689-residue bilobal iron-binding glycoprotein with sixteen disulfide bonds and five N-glycosylation sites. It is antimicrobial, commercially attractive as a high-value ingredient, and biophysically the most demanding target in the panel — which is exactly why it is included. If folding and modification load rather than metabolic cost is what limits production, lactoferrin is where that should be most visible.

Its production history in this host is unusually well documented for a food protein. Recombinant human lactoferrin has been expressed in glyco-engineered *P. pastoris* with terminal N-acetylneuraminic acid (Choi et al. 2008, 10.1007/s10719-008-9123-y), and the regulatory-science literature around the *K. phaffii*-produced human protein is, as noted in §2.1.2, further advanced than for any other protein in the panel. The species distinction matters and is a trap the corpus had to be built to avoid: published titres for human lactoferrin and bovine lactoferrin differ by roughly an order of magnitude, and an analysis that joins titres to sequences on the name "lactoferrin" will silently attribute one species' titre to the other's sequence. §3.8 and Appendix F record that this happened once and how it was caught.

### 2.4.4 Ovalbumin

Ovalbumin is a 386-residue serpin-family protein and the dominant protein of egg white. It is an unusual case in two respects. First, it is secreted without cleavage of an N-terminal signal peptide, carrying instead an internal non-cleaved signal — a feature that §3.5 records as one of the five UniProt-parsing decisions that had to be made correctly, because treating the annotated signal peptide as cleaved would have removed 27 residues from the mature chain that are in fact present. Second, it carries a modest modification load for its size: one disulfide bond, one N-glycosylation site and two phosphoserines.

Recombinant production data are sparse. Essentially one primary experimental source reports a titre, in *T. reesei* (Aro et al. 2023), alongside company life-cycle assessments and a demonstration of secretion in *K. phaffii* that reports no titre at all. Egg-white proteins and their food applications are reviewed by Abeyrathne and colleagues (2013, 10.3382/ps.2013-03391) and, more recently, with attention to modification strategies and techno-functional application (Usturoi et al. 2026, 10.3390/foods15213817). The scientific and technological challenges of recombinant egg protein production specifically have been reviewed by Beck and colleagues (2025, 10.1186/s12896-025-01002-3).

Ovalbumin is also, as §5.2.2 reports, the protein with the largest *within-protein* spread in the whole corpus: four qualifying records spanning 200-fold. That makes it the clearest single illustration of the thesis's central finding, which is that the same protein in the same host reaches very different titres in different hands.

### 2.4.5 Soy leghemoglobin

Leghemoglobin C2 is a 145-residue plant globin that binds one haem b, and it is the only cofactor-dependent protein in the panel. Production in yeast therefore requires the host to supply protohaem at a rate far above its own needs, because a yeast's native haem demand is set by its cytochromes and catalases rather than by a product present at grams per litre.

The published route to high titre is precisely that. High-level secretory production of leghemoglobin in *P. pastoris* was achieved through enhanced globin expression combined with remodelling of haem biosynthesis (Shao et al. 2022, 10.1016/j.biortech.2022.127884), and the same logic — balanced globin expression and haem biosynthesis — had earlier been established for human haemoglobin in *S. cerevisiae* (Liu et al. 2014, 10.1016/j.ymben.2013.10.010). Haemoglobin yield in yeast has also been improved by engineering its degradation rather than its synthesis (Ishchuk et al. 2021, 10.1016/j.ymben.2021.05.002), which is a reminder that for a cofactor-binding protein, stability of the holoprotein and supply of the cofactor are separate problems. High-level leghemoglobin expression has been achieved in *Kluyveromyces marxianus* by remodelling haem metabolism (Tian et al. 2024, 10.3389/fbioe.2023.1329016), and metabolic engineering of *S. cerevisiae* for haem itself establishes the pathway as an engineering target in its own right (Lee et al. 2024, 10.1002/biot.202400351). Growth-coupled haem biosynthesis in *P. pastoris* has been analysed with metabolic modelling (Pentjuss et al. 2023, 10.1038/s41598-023-42865-w), which is the closest prior art to the leghemoglobin part of Chapter 4. Production and purification from different expression media is documented by Bolmanis and colleagues (2023, 10.3390/pr11113215), and the protein's structure, production, safety and food applications are reviewed by Ahmad and colleagues (2023, 10.1016/j.tifs.2023.104199).

Chapter 4 quantifies the stoichiometric part of the haem constraint and finds it real but modest. That finding places the weight on flux capacity and catalysis rather than on carbon and energy — which is the same distinction the caseins force, in a different pathway, and it is the reason §7 treats haem supply as a flux problem rather than a yield problem.

### 2.4.6 What the panel spans, and why that matters

Taken together the panel spans intrinsic disorder against tight globular folds; zero to sixteen disulfide bonds; zero to five N-glycosylation sites; zero to thirteen phosphorylation sites; one cofactor requirement; and a nearly sixfold range of chain length. That spread is what makes the comparative analysis informative, because it means the stoichiometric ceiling is being computed across most of the property space a food-protein developer would encounter.

It is also what makes the central result surprising. A panel spanning that much structural and modificational variation might reasonably be expected to span a wide range of metabolic cost. §4 and §5 report that it does not: the ceiling varies by 1.31-fold across 26 proteins, with a log₁₀ standard deviation of 0.029. The property space is wide; the cost space is narrow. Everything in this thesis follows from that.

## 2.5 Computational methods used in this field

The methods cluster of the bibliography — 49 references, the largest single cluster — covers the tools this thesis uses, the tools it deliberately declines to use, and the reasons in both cases. The guiding principle in assembling it was that a method's suitability depends on the object it is applied to, and that several of the field's standard tools are applied, in the literature, to objects they were not built for.

### 2.5.1 Structure and disorder

AlphaFold made high-confidence structure prediction routine (Jumper et al. 2021, 10.1038/s41586-021-03819-2), AlphaFold 3 extended it to biomolecular interactions (Abramson et al. 2024, 10.1038/s41586-024-07487-w, with addendum 10.1038/s41586-024-08416-7), and protein language models extended single-sequence inference to evolutionary scale (Lin et al. 2023, 10.1126/science.ade2574). The consequences for protein design and engineering are reviewed widely: structure-prediction networks as foundation models (Wang et al. 2024, 10.1101/cshperspect.a041472), emerging frontiers after the AlphaFold revolution (Rennie 2025, 10.1098/rsif.2024.0886), advantages and limitations from recent studies (Li et al. 2025, 10.1007/s10930-025-10310-8), conformational ensembles for structure prediction (Yang et al. 2025, 10.1038/s41598-024-84066-z), and zero-shot prediction of design success by AlphaFold, ESMFold and ProteinMPNN (Garcia et al. 2026, 10.1002/pro.70453). ProteinMPNN itself (Dauparas et al. 2022, 10.1126/science.add2187) is the standard inverse-folding tool.

For the panel in this thesis, however, structure predictors are partly the wrong instrument, and the reason is the caseins. A confident structure prediction of an intrinsically disordered protein is a confident prediction of something that does not exist as a single state. The appropriate reference tools are dedicated disorder predictors — IUPred3 (Erdős et al. 2021, 10.1093/nar/gkab408), flDPnn (Hu et al. 2021, 10.1038/s41467-021-24773-7) and metapredict (Emenecker et al. 2021, 10.1016/j.bpj.2021.08.039) — together with the comparative evaluation of AlphaFold2 against them (Zhao and Kurgan 2023, 10.1016/j.csbj.2023.06.001) and the systematic identification of *conditionally* folded disordered regions by AlphaFold2 (Alderson et al. 2023, 10.1073/pnas.2304302120), which is the paper that shows the two classes of tool are measuring related but distinct things.

This thesis computes disorder with metapredict (version 3.1.01), on the mature chain after signal-peptide removal, and retains the dependency-free charge–hydropathy FoldIndex as a transparent fallback. The choice of statistic turned out to matter more than the choice of predictor: a *fraction* of residues above a disorder threshold is identically zero for every globular protein in the panel, while the *mean* score retains the gradient among them, and the two give very different correlations with ceiling utilisation. §6.5 records it.

### 2.5.2 Post-translational modification prediction

Phosphorylation-site prediction has a long tool history: NetPhos (Blom et al. 1999, 10.1006/jmbi.1999.3310), NetPhosPan (Fenoy et al. 2019, 10.1093/bioinformatics/bty715), GPS 6.0 (Chen et al. 2023, 10.1093/nar/gkad383), KinasePhos 3.0 (Ma et al. 2023, 10.1016/j.gpb.2022.06.004) and MusiteDeep (Wang et al. 2020, 10.1093/nar/gkaa275), with the machine-learning methods reviewed by Esmaili and colleagues (2023, 10.1016/j.gpb.2023.03.007). N-glycosylation site prediction has a parallel lineage: sequon scanning with structural properties and patterns (Chuang et al. 2012, 10.1093/bioinformatics/bts426), N-GlyDE (Pitti et al. 2019, 10.1038/s41598-019-52341-z), DeepNGlyPred (Pakhrin et al. 2021, 10.3390/molecules26237314), LMNglyPred using protein language model embeddings (Pakhrin et al. 2023, 10.1093/glycob/cwad033), and the NetNGlyc server.

This thesis uses **annotated UniProt modification sites in preference to predicted ones** for the panel, and sequon counts for the wider validation set. That asymmetry is declared in Chapter 3 because it is a real inconsistency: the panel's modification counts are annotation-grade and the validation set's are prediction-grade, and any comparison across that boundary is weaker than a comparison within either side of it. §3.5 records the five specific parsing decisions that annotation-grade counting required, each of which would have produced a materially wrong number if taken the obvious way — the distinction between alternate glycoforms and alternate disulfide pairings, inter-chain bonds stored as point features on each partner, glycation entries that are not glycosylation, and signal peptides annotated as not cleaved.

### 2.5.3 Signal peptides, localisation and aggregation

SignalP 6.0 (Teufel et al. 2022, 10.1038/s41587-021-01156-3) and DeepLoc 2.0 (Thumuluri et al. 2022, 10.1093/nar/gkac278) are the standard tools for signal peptide and localisation prediction. They are discussed here but not run, because every construct designed in Chapter 4 uses a single common secretion leader, which removes signal-peptide choice as a variable rather than predicting it.

Aggregation propensity is a richer case. The field has a mature toolset: TANGO-style prediction of sequence-dependent and mutational effects (Fernandez-Escamilla et al. 2004, 10.1038/nbt1012), AGGRESCAN (Conchillo-Solé et al. 2007, 10.1186/1471-2105-8-65) and its structural and pH-dependent successors AGGRESCAN3D (Zambrano et al. 2015, 10.1093/nar/gkv359) and Aggrescan4D (Zalewski et al. 2024, 10.1002/pro.5180), CamSol for solubility design (Sormanni et al. 2015, 10.1016/j.jmb.2014.09.026) and its pH-dependent extension (Oeller et al. 2023, 10.1093/bib/bbad004), and AggreProt for predicting and engineering aggregation-prone regions (Planas-Iglesias et al. 2024, 10.1093/nar/gkae420). The area is reviewed repeatedly (Santos et al. 2020, 10.1016/j.csbj.2020.05.026; Navarro and Ventura 2022, 10.1016/j.sbi.2022.102343; Pesce et al. 2026, 10.1007/s12551-025-01404-9).

Aggregation propensity would be a natural addition to this thesis's feature panel, and it is not used. The reason is sample size rather than scepticism: the feature panel was fixed at seven before the analysis was run, against a corpus that yields 88 qualifying records, and adding features to a model that §6.4 shows has negative skill from sequence features would not be a test of anything. It is listed as future work in Chapter 9.

### 2.5.4 Codon usage, and a caution the field has already issued

The codon adaptation index of Sharp and Li (1987, 10.1093/nar/15.3.1281) remains the standard measure of codon adaptation, with a theoretical re-examination (Jansen and colleagues, 2003 — see the identifier note in the bibliography) and a test for translational selection by dos Reis and colleagues (2004, 10.1093/nar/gkh834) establishing its statistical foundations. Codon-based indices for modelling expression and transcript evolution are reviewed by Bahiri-Elitzur and colleagues (2021, 10.1016/j.csbj.2021.04.042).

In this host specifically, codon usage bias has been shown to regulate both gene expression and protein conformation (Xu et al. 2021, 10.1186/s12934-021-01580-9), codon pair optimisation has been implemented as a design tool for *P. pastoris* (Huang et al. 2021, 10.1186/s12934-021-01696-y), synonymous codon usage bias was examined for human glucocerebrosidase expression in this yeast as early as 2002 (Sinclair and Elliott, 10.1016/s1046-5928(02)00526-0), and codon optimisation has recently been shown to affect expression by altering chromatin accessibility (Lu et al. 2025, 10.3390/jof11040282) — a mechanism entirely outside the translational-efficiency framework that codon indices assume. Codon optimisation as a mathematical programming problem has also been formalised (2020, *Bioinformatics*).

Two cautions from this literature are adopted in Chapter 3 rather than argued with. The first is Mauro and Chappell's critical analysis of codon optimisation in human therapeutics (2014, 10.1016/j.molmed.2014.09.003), which documents how maximal-CAI designs can impair function. The second is more specific to this panel: Liu and colleagues (2022, 10.1016/j.jbiotec.2021.11.001) report a strong *negative* correlation between codon usage bias and protein structural disorder, and that codon optimisation can impede expression of disordered proteins. Since the caseins are the disordered proteins in this panel and the central test case of the thesis, that finding is directly on point, and it is the reason Chapter 3 proposes *harmonised* rather than maximal-CAI designs for synthesis. A large-scale empirical study of 11,430 recombinant production experiments found that yield is tunable by synonymous codon changes at translation initiation sites specifically (Bhandari et al. 2021, 10.1371/journal.pcbi.1009461), which locates the effect more precisely than a whole-gene index can.

Chapter 3 also records a correction to this thesis's own earlier practice. A proper CAI requires a reference set of highly expressed genes, not a whole-genome codon table; the analysis was rebuilt against a 165-gene highly expressed reference derived from the host's RefSeq annotation, and §3.7 reports what changed.

## 2.6 Genome-scale metabolic models and the cost of a protein

### 2.6.1 Models of this host

Four generations of genome-scale model exist for *K. phaffii*. PpaMBEL1254 and the first genome-scale model of the organism, used for in silico analysis of heterologous protein production, were published by Sohn and colleagues (2010, 10.1002/biot.201000078); iLC915 and the Cambridge Kp1.0 reconstruction followed; the iMT1026 lineage became the de facto standard; and the recent iPichia consensus model with its subsequent gene–protein–reaction curation represents the current frontier (Kocabaş 2026, 10.1016/j.bej.2025.109940 and 10.1002/bit.70402).

This thesis uses **iMT1026v3** (Tomàs-Gamisans et al. 2018, 10.1111/1751-7915.12871), which fine-tunes the iMT1026 reconstruction for improved prediction of growth on methanol or glycerol as sole carbon sources and carries separate biomass equations for each. That feature is directly load-bearing: the carbon-source dependence of the yield ceiling is the largest single effect Chapter 4 reports, and a model without carbon-source-specific biomass equations could not resolve it. The choice is conservative rather than novel — a newer consensus model exists — and Chapter 9 states the trade: iMT1026v3 is the version with the most published validation for exactly the growth conditions this analysis assumes.

Related modelling in this host includes contextualised models revealing high-order metabolic effects of growth rate and oxygenation (Torres et al. 2019), comparative genome-scale analysis used to select an optimal base strain (Brady et al. 2020, 10.1002/bit.27209), a constraint-based estimate of recombinant protein production (Tortajada et al. 2012, 10.1016/j.jprocont.2012.03.009), growth-coupled haem biosynthesis analysis (Pentjuss et al. 2023), and the glycosylation-aware model already discussed (Irani et al. 2016). The computational work was carried out with COBRApy (Ebrahim et al. 2013, 10.1186/1752-0509-7-74).

### 2.6.2 The proteome-constrained frontier

A stoichiometric model does not know that an enzyme occupies space or that a ribosome has a finite rate. The modelling tradition that fixes this — enzymatic and proteome constraints — is the methodological frontier for the present question, and this thesis is explicit that it sits one generation behind it.

GECKO introduced enzymatic constraints to a yeast genome-scale model with substantial gains in phenotype prediction (Sánchez et al. 2017, 10.15252/msb.20167411); ME-models integrated metabolism and gene expression (O'Brien et al. 2013, 10.1038/msb.2013.52); and pcSecYeast applied proteome constraints specifically to improving recombinant protein production predictions (Li et al. 2022, 10.1038/s41467-022-30689-7). Omic data from evolved *E. coli* were shown to be consistent with computed optimal growth from genome-scale models (Lewis et al. 2010, 10.1038/msb.2010.47), which is the empirical warrant for treating a computed optimum as a meaningful bound at all.

**This matters for the interpretation of the central result.** A stoichiometric ceiling is a bound on carbon and energy, not on machinery. Finding that published titres reach under 8% of a stoichiometric ceiling is therefore entirely consistent with those titres sitting at or near a *proteome* ceiling that this analysis cannot compute. Chapter 9 identifies porting this decomposition onto a proteome-constrained *K. phaffii* model as the single most valuable extension of the work, precisely because that is the calculation that would distinguish "nobody has tried hard enough" from "the machinery is full".

### 2.6.3 The cost of a protein, as a concept

The conceptual foundation is Akashi and Gojobori's demonstration that amino acids differ systematically in biosynthetic cost and that proteomes are composed accordingly (2002, 10.1073/pnas.062526999), extended to yeast specifically in an evolutionary systems-biology framing (Barton et al. 2010, 10.1371/journal.pone.0011935) and, recently, tied to abundance and stability: amino acid sequence encodes protein abundance shaped by stability at reduced synthesis cost (Buric et al. 2025, 10.1002/pro.5239).

Two papers compute per-protein costs in a model and are the direct prior art for Chapter 4. Feizi and colleagues modelled the protein secretory machinery in *S. cerevisiae* at genome scale and computed per-protein costs in ATP, GTP, GDP-mannose and UDP-GlcNAc across the proteome (2013, 10.1371/journal.pone.0063284). Gutierrez and colleagues reconstructed the mammalian secretory pathway and computed per-protein metabolic costs and limits of secretion, together with the features most affecting it (2020, 10.1038/s41467-019-13867-y). The supply-chain framing of protein production and secretion in yeasts and mammalian cells (Klein et al. 2015, 10.1007/s10295-014-1569-2) is the conceptual bridge between the cost literature and the host-engineering literature.

**Chapter 4 is an application of this prior work to a panel it did not cover, and is presented as such.** §2.8 states this concession formally.

## 2.7 Techno-economics and sustainability

### 2.7.1 The state of public cost modelling

Published cost estimates for precision-fermented protein diverge enormously, and the divergence is a finding in itself. The Good Food Institute's meta-analysis of public techno-economic models (2025) found figures spanning three orders of magnitude, driven by differing assumptions rather than by differing biology. Its European arm's 2025 analysis placed food-grade α-lactalbumin near $24 kg⁻¹ with high-purity grades near $100 kg⁻¹. For reference, commodity whey protein trades at a few dollars per kilogram, and the benchmark repeatedly cited for commodity competitiveness is under $20 kg⁻¹. Trade analysis notes that only four public cost models exist for this product class and that none sits where the industry reports itself to be, and separately that feedstock is not half the cost of precision fermentation — yield is the number that matters.

Peer-reviewed techno-economic analysis specific to precision fermentation of *food* proteins barely exists, and this is a real gap rather than a search failure. The closest rigorous anchors are adjacent. Humbird's scale-up economics for cultured meat (2021, 10.1002/bit.27848) treats bioreactor capital, oxygen transfer and media cost in a way that transfers directly to this problem and is, on the evidence of the corpus, the most quantitatively careful public document in the adjacent space. The single-cell protein literature supplies several recent quantitative analyses: gas fermentation for single-cell protein (Jean et al. 2024, 10.1021/acs.est.3c10312), single-cell protein from industrial off-gas through acetate (Vlaeminck et al. 2023, 10.3390/fermentation9080771), the global potential of single-cell protein on variable renewable electricity (Fasihi et al. 2025, 10.1038/s41467-025-56364-1), and techno-economic assessment at industrial scale across a wide range of carbon sources (Almeida Benalcázar et al. 2026, 10.1016/j.cej.2026.180714). The role of techno-economic and life-cycle assessment in guiding the field is itself reviewed (David et al. 2026, 10.1016/j.tifs.2025.105488).

Chapter 8 of this thesis builds a deliberately transparent model rather than a comprehensive one, for the reason the meta-analysis makes plain: in a space where published estimates span three orders of magnitude because of assumptions, the useful contribution is a model whose assumptions are all visible and whose sensitivity to each is reported.

### 2.7.2 Downstream processing, which is where the cost is

Downstream processing is reviewed separately and is repeatedly identified as the cost driver for food-grade products (Keppler et al. 2026, 10.1146/annurev-food-060424-091647). The reason is structural: chromatography designed for pharmaceutical purity is not affordable at food prices, and a food protein must therefore be purified by operations borrowed from food processing rather than from biologics. A demonstrated alternative is polyphosphate precipitation of β-lactoglobulin from *P. pastoris*, which raised protein content from 26 to 72 weight per cent while preserving emulsifying function — the kind of unit operation that makes a food-grade cost structure plausible.

This is directly relevant to the thesis's central measurement. If downstream cost scales with volume processed rather than with product mass, then titre is the dominant lever on cost per kilogram, and a protein stuck at 0.06% of its stoichiometric ceiling is expensive for reasons that have nothing to do with its amino acid composition. Chapter 8 quantifies that relationship.

### 2.7.3 Life-cycle assessment

Life-cycle assessments report large reductions in greenhouse-gas emissions, water use and land use relative to bovine milk protein, but the reported range is wide and strongly dependent on the assumed electricity mix. Peer-reviewed environmental impact assessment of dairy protein from precision fermentation is now available (Quandt et al. 2026, 10.1007/s11367-026-02765-3), alongside company-produced assessments for ovalbumin in *T. reesei* (Onego Bio 2024) and an institutional analysis of egg-white protein (VTT 2022). Dissenting and comparative analyses exist in the trade and advocacy literature, including direct comparisons of precision-fermentation life-cycle assessments against one another and critical treatments of bioreactor environmental impact.

Chapter 8 reports ranges and dissent rather than a single headline figure, because the spread between published assessments is larger than the difference any result in this thesis would make to one.

Broader reviews place all of this in context: biotechnology in dairy alternatives and fermentation technologies for replacing milk (Purba et al. 2025, 10.5851/kosfa.2025.e33), next-generation precision fermentation integrating engineered cell factories and bioprocess intensification (Mahmoud et al. 2026, 10.1007/s00253-026-14051-1), the recombinant microbial production of milk and egg proteins (Nielsen et al. 2024, 10.1146/annurev-food-072023-034256), whether recombinant milk proteins can replace animal ones (Hettinga and colleagues 2022, 10.1016/j.copbio.2022.102690), and precision fermentation as an alternative to animal protein (Knychala et al. 2024, 10.3390/fermentation10060315).

## 2.8 What has already been done, conceded plainly

This thesis was preceded by a systematic prior-art audit of roughly 220 screened records with verified identifiers. The audit's full detail, including the searches run and the records screened, is in **Appendix G**; what belongs in the body of the chapter is the concession, and it is short.

**Per-protein biosynthetic cost in a metabolic model is established work.** Feizi and colleagues (2013) computed per-protein ATP, GTP and glycosylation-donor costs across the yeast proteome from essentially the same features used here. Gutierrez and colleagues (2020) reconstructed the mammalian secretory pathway and computed per-protein secretion costs and limits. This step is not novel and is not claimed as such; it is used as an instrument, with citation, in Chapters 3 and 4.

**Protein-specific secretion reactions with bottleneck attribution are established work.** pcSecYeast (Li et al. 2022) builds protein-specific secretion reactions from sequence and post-translational requirements for eight recombinant proteins, including a haemoglobin, and makes explicit per-protein bottleneck calls. This is the closest methodological prior art to Chapter 6, and it is cited as the method's origin rather than competed with.

**Predicting producibility from sequence is a developed field, including in fungi.** Presep (Tian et al. 2013) predicts secretion propensity specifically in *P. pastoris*; the phrase "predicting protein producibility" was used for filamentous fungi in 2017 (Dykstra, Rousu and Arvas, 10.1101/138560); and large-scale empirical analyses populate the space thoroughly — 11,430 production experiments (Bhandari et al. 2021), determinants across the human secretome (Masson et al. 2025), deep protein representations for expression prediction (Martiny et al. 2021), ExpressUrself (Dunne et al. 2022), PERISCOPE-Opt (Packiam et al. 2022) and high-throughput developability assays at library scale (Golinski et al. 2021) — while the biologics developability-index tradition (Lauer et al. 2012) establishes composite sequence-based scores as a legitimate construct. **A "producibility index" is therefore not a novel object, and this thesis does not claim one.** An earlier framing did; the audit retired it, and §6.4 and §5.2.2 supply the quantitative reason it could not have worked on this data anyway.

**A qualitative tractability ranking of milk proteins is already in print.** A 2026 review (Guo et al., 10.1111/1541-4337.70618) states that α-lactalbumin and β-lactoglobulin are the most tractable targets given minimal post-translational-modification dependency, and places caseins and lactoferrin in a harder tier. The ordering this thesis might otherwise have claimed to discover is therefore published already — as an expert judgement, with no score, no stoichiometry, no model and no validation against titres. §2.9 turns this into a design feature rather than a problem.

**Concurrent independent work exists on the same host and model.** A publicly visible, unpublished repository implements translation, glycosylation, disulfide and COPII cost builders on iMT1026v3 in *K. phaffii* with a per-protein nutrient-cost pipeline. No associated paper was found. It is recorded here because that is the honest description of the ground.

**What the audit did not find, and what is claimed here.** No retrieved work computes, for a panel of named food proteins in *K. phaffii* or *T. reesei*, the maximum theoretical yield of each protein, expresses each published titre as a fraction of *its own* protein's ceiling, and decomposes the residual — nor, more importantly, reports that about half the dispersion in realised production is reproduced within single proteins at fixed sequence. The components exist. This assembly, the measurement it produces, and the corpus that makes it checkable do not.

## 2.9 What the review implies for the design of this thesis

Three consequences follow, and all three are adopted.

**The prior art is conceded at the point of use.** Chapters 3 and 4 present the synthesis-cost machinery as an application of Feizi, Gutierrez, Irani and pcSecYeast to a panel they did not cover, not as an invention.

**The contribution is narrowed to the measurement and the corpus**: the ceiling; each published titre as a fraction of its own protein's ceiling; the finding that about half the dispersion lives within single proteins; and the 306-record quotation-backed corpus that makes all of it auditable.

**The published expert ordering becomes a pre-registered test rather than a competitor.** If this analysis recovers that ordering from first principles, that is a validation of the method — provided the expectation is declared before looking. It is declared here: **the literature predicts whey proteins easiest, caseins and lactoferrin hardest.** Chapter 6 reports what the data say, including where they contradict it, which is human α-lactalbumin.


# Chapter 3 — Materials and methods

Everything in this chapter is computational. The governing principle is that any number appearing later in this thesis must be regenerable by running code in the accompanying repository against data files whose provenance is recorded. Where that principle could not be met — and there are three such places, all caused by network restrictions on the analysis machine — the compromise is stated explicitly here rather than buried.

## 3.1 Software environment

Analyses were performed in Python 3.13 using COBRApy 0.32.1 for constraint-based modelling with the GLPK linear-programming solver, Biopython for sequence analysis, pandas and NumPy for data handling, scikit-learn for regression and cross-validation, SciPy for statistics, and Matplotlib for figures. No commercial software, no graphics-processing hardware and no cloud compute service was used; the entire analysis runs on a four-core machine in under an hour.

## 3.2 The metabolic model

The genome-scale metabolic model **iMT1026v3** of *Komagataella phaffii* was used throughout (Tomàs-Gamisans et al. 2017, 10.1111/1751-7915.12871). As loaded, it contains 2,237 reactions, 1,706 metabolites and 1,026 genes. Two properties made it the right choice over the alternatives.

First, it carries **separate biomass and macromolecular composition equations for growth on glycerol and on methanol** (BIOMASS_glyc and BIOMASS_meoh, with corresponding PROTEINS_glyc and PROTEINS_meoh amino-acid composition reactions). Since the carbon-source effect turns out to be the largest single effect in Chapter 4, a model that treats methanol growth with its own composition and maintenance parameters is not a convenience but a requirement. The growth-associated maintenance term differs substantially between them (68.7 against 166.8 mmol ATP per gram of biomass), which encodes the known energetic penalty of methanol dissimilation.

Second, it is distributed as standard SBML and loads without curation into COBRApy, which keeps the pipeline reproducible.

The earlier iMT1026 reconstruction and the iLC915 model are included in the repository for comparison but are not used for the reported results.

## 3.3 Target proteins and sequence provenance

### 3.3.1 The panel

Nine proteins were analysed: bovine α-S1-casein (UniProt P02662), α-S2-casein (P02663), β-casein (P02666) and κ-casein (P02668); bovine β-lactoglobulin (P02754) and α-lactalbumin (P00711); hen ovalbumin (P01012); soybean leghemoglobin C2 (P02236); and bovine lactoferrin (P24627).

### 3.3.2 Sequence verification, and why it was necessary

The analysis machine could not reach UniProt, NCBI, EBI or RCSB; only GitHub and package registries were accessible. Sequences were therefore recovered from UniProt-derived FASTA files mirrored in public repositories, which creates an obvious risk: a mirrored file may carry a truncated, variant or mislabelled record.

That risk was closed by a verification rule applied before any sequence entered the analysis. For each protein, the UniProt entry's stated sequence length and molecular mass were obtained from the entry's own text record, and the sequence recovered from the mirror was accepted **only if its length matched exactly and its molecular mass, recomputed from average residue masses, agreed to within 1 Da per 10 kDa**. All nine panel proteins passed. The check is implemented in `src/build_targets.py` and re-runs on every build, so a corrupted data file causes a loud failure rather than a quiet wrong answer.

For the wider validation set of Chapter 6, a second rule was used: a sequence was accepted when **two or more independent repositories carried byte-identical records** for the same accession. Of 24 accessions harvested this way, 23 met the two-repository consensus criterion and one is flagged as single-source in the manifest.

This is a workaround and is declared as one. With direct database access the same pipeline runs unchanged against the REST endpoint, and re-verification against UniProt is listed in Chapter 9 as a required pre-submission task.

### 3.3.3 Annotation

Signal peptide boundaries, disulfide bond counts, N-glycosylation sites, phosphorylation sites and cofactor requirements were taken from the UniProt feature table of each entry. For the nine-member target panel they are recorded in `src/targets.py` with the position lists; for all 36 accessions in the wider validation corpus they are extracted programmatically in `src/uniprot_annotations.py` from the locally stored, CRC64-verified entries.

Parsing a UniProt feature table correctly turned out to require five explicit decisions, each of which was wrong in a first implementation and each of which inflated a protein's apparent modification load. They are recorded because every one of them changes a computed ceiling.

- **"Alternate" means opposite things on different feature types, and this is the subtlety that bit both implementations of this pipeline.** On a `Glycosylation` feature it marks an alternative *glycoform at an occupied site*, so the site is real and the extra lines are duplicates of one site. On a `Disulfide bond` feature it marks an alternative *pairing*, so keeping it invents a bond that does not coexist with the one beside it. In both cases the alternate lines are dropped, but for opposite reasons, and an implementation that applies one rule to both will be wrong on one of them.
- **An alternate annotation is not an extra feature.** β-lactoglobulin lists three DISULFID features (82–176, 122–135, 122–137), the last marked *Alternate*: it is the same cysteine pair in an alternative pairing, so the protein has two disulfides and one free cysteine. The same rule applies to glycosylation: UniProt writes one line per observed glycoform, so bovine lactoferrin's **five** N-glycosylation sites appear as **nine** lines, Asn-387 and Asn-495 each carrying three alternate entries for complex, high-mannose and hybrid glycans. All counts are therefore over distinct sequence positions, not over feature lines.
- **An inter-chain disulfide is stored twice.** UniProt records it as a point feature on each of the two partner cysteines. Bovine κ-casein's entry carries one intramolecular bond (Cys-32–Cys-109) plus two point features describing that same pair in the casein polymer; counted naively it has three bonds. Point features are counted once as inter-chain and reported separately.
- **Glycation is not glycosylation.** Human serum albumin's entry carries 24 `Glycosylation` lines reading "N-linked (Glc) (glycation) lysine", several marked *in vitro*. These are non-enzymatic glycation of lysine, not oligosaccharyltransferase products; albumin is not a glycoprotein. Charging it 24 glycans depressed its computed ceiling by a third. A site is counted only if it is N-linked, is not glycation, and sits on an asparagine in the sequence itself.
- **A signal peptide marked "Not cleaved" is not a cleavage site.** Ovalbumin's Signal feature spans residues 22–48 and is annotated *Not cleaved*: it is an internal signal sequence and the mature protein is the whole chain. Treating 48 as a cleavage point would have deleted an eighth of the protein.
- **An assumption is not a measurement.** Where no annotation was available the earlier analysis assumed cysteines pair, ⌊Cys/2⌋. Applied to the 36 annotated accessions, that assumption is wrong for **15 of them**, by up to eight bonds, and it over-assigns bonds specifically to cytosolic and fibrous proteins whose cysteines are free. It survives only as a declared fallback, and §6.2.1 reports what it did to the one positive result of the first draft.

The extractor was validated against the hand-curated nine-protein panel: it reproduces 43 of 45 annotation fields, and the two disagreements were both errors in the hand curation, since corrected (κ-casein's one intramolecular disulfide and three phosphoserine positions). The panel still verifies 9 of 9 against UniProt length and mass.

- **Disulfide bond formation**: 0.5 O₂ consumed and one H₂O released per bond, representing the Ero1–PDI relay that passes electrons from the forming disulfide to molecular oxygen.
- **N-glycosylation**: per occupied sequon, 2 UDP-GlcNAc and *m* GDP-mannose consumed, releasing UDP and GDP. The default *m* = 9 corresponds to the core Man₈GlcNAc₂ transferred in the endoplasmic reticulum, which is the conservative floor; Golgi outer-chain extension is scanned at *m* ∈ {9, 20, 60, 100} to represent the hypermannosylation characteristic of this host.
- **Phosphorylation**: one ATP → ADP per phosphoserine.
- **Haem**: one protohaem IX per chain for leghemoglobin. iMT1026v3 synthesises protohaem only in the mitochondrial compartment (pheme_m), so an explicit mitochondrial export reaction was added to make the cofactor available to a cytosolic apoprotein. This addition is physiologically real but is flagged, since it is a modification to the published model.

A demand reaction then removes the finished protein from the system, standing in for secretion and harvest.

### 3.4.1 Product mass: a correction worth recording

An early version of this analysis charged the cell for glycans, phosphate and haem but computed the yield using the mass of the **polypeptide alone**. That is inconsistent: the modification mass is paid for and leaves the cell, so it must be credited somewhere. The error was found by an independent reimplementation of the same engine, which computed bovine lactoferrin's product mass as 85.44 kDa against the 76.14 kDa polypeptide — a 9.3 kDa discrepancy exactly equal to five core glycans.

The resolution adopted is to compute and report **both** yields:

- **Product-basis yield**, in grams of the secreted molecule including its modifications per gram of substrate (glycan residues at 203.195 Da for HexNAc and 162.142 Da for hexose, phosphate at 79.980 Da, haem b at 616.487 Da, less 2.016 Da per disulfide).
- **Polypeptide-basis yield**, counting only the backbone.

These answer different questions and diverge sharply under hypermannosylation, which is itself one of the findings of Chapter 4. Where a single number is needed — in particular when comparing against published titres, which are measured as protein — the **polypeptide basis** is used, because it is the conservative choice and because mannan is not what a food application is buying.

## 3.5 Yield computation

The substrate exchange reaction was constrained to 10 mmol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹ for the carbon source under test, all other carbon exchanges were closed, oxygen was left unconstrained, and the non-growth maintenance requirement of the model (2.9 mmol ATP gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹) was retained. Biomass formation was fixed at zero for the maximum-yield case — the theoretical ceiling assumes no cell growth — and at a series of fixed non-zero rates for the production-versus-growth envelope. The protein demand reaction was then maximised by flux balance analysis.

Yields are reported as grams of protein per gram of substrate, as carbon moles of product per carbon mole of substrate, and as the substrate mass required per gram of protein. Carbon dioxide and oxygen exchange at the optimum are recorded alongside, since they bound the heat and aeration duties used in Chapter 8.

Three carbon sources were analysed: glycerol (the standard biomass-accumulation substrate for this host), methanol (the inducer for AOX1-driven expression) and glucose.

## 3.6 Sequence feature panel

Features were computed in `src/features.py`: length and molecular mass; isoelectric point, GRAVY hydropathy, aromaticity and instability index from Biopython's ProtParam; mean net charge per residue and fraction of charged residues; cysteine count, annotated disulfide count and disulfides per 100 residues; N-glycosylation sequons; annotated phosphosites; and amino-acid composition as twenty fractions.

**Disorder** is estimated by FoldIndex (Prilusky et al. 2005), *2.785⟨H⟩ − |⟨R⟩| − 1.151*, where ⟨H⟩ is mean Kyte–Doolittle hydropathy scaled to the unit interval and ⟨R⟩ is mean net charge per residue, evaluated both globally and in a 51-residue sliding window to give a disordered fraction and the longest disordered run. A neural consensus disorder predictor (metapredict v3) is used for the reported disorder values, computed on the mature chain after signal-peptide removal, which matters: including the signal peptide shifts the mean score enough to change a rank correlation's magnitude (§6.5.2). Both a *mean* score and a *fraction of residues above 0.5* are recorded, and §6.5.1 reports why the choice between them turned out to be load-bearing; FoldIndex is retained and reported alongside as the transparent, dependency-free fallback, and the pipeline falls back to it automatically when the neural predictor is unavailable. An earlier version of this analysis ran on FoldIndex alone because the predictor could not be installed; both sets of values are in `results/tables/target_features.csv`, and the conclusions of Chapter 6 are reported for the neural predictor.

## 3.7 Codon optimisation

Codon usage was computed from the complete coding-sequence set of the RefSeq *K. phaffii* GS115 assembly (GCF_000027005.1, ASM2700v1): **5,040 coding sequences, 2,399,341 sense codons**, with the download archive's checksum recorded. Two tables were derived, giving per codon the count, the synonymous fraction, the frequency per thousand, the RSCU and the relative adaptiveness *w<sub>i</sub>*.

The reference set is the load-bearing choice. Classical CAI (Sharp and Li 1987) measures how closely a gene's codon choice resembles that of **highly expressed** genes, not the genomic average; against a genome-average table every native gene scores near the middle by construction and the index carries little information. No expression data for this organism was available, so the reference set was assembled from the standard proxy classes: **165 genes** comprising 129 cytosolic ribosomal proteins, 23 translation factors, 6 chaperones, 6 glycolytic enzymes and alcohol oxidase, all listed in `data/external/kphaffii_cds/highly_expressed_genes.tsv`.

Assembling that set by free-text matching on product names is a trap, and it was fallen into first. Matching "ubiquitin" admitted 55 genes that were ligases, conjugating enzymes and proteases, together with actin-associated and Rho-GAP proteins — all regulatory, all low-abundance. With those included, the reference set separated from the genomic average by only 0.050 in mean *w*; after exclusion the separation is 0.135, nearly three times larger. Contaminating a CAI reference with average-usage genes flattens the index towards the genomic average and quietly destroys its discriminating power.

The consequence is visible in the designs themselves. Scoring the same nine maximum-CAI designs against the same nine uniform-synonymous controls, the separation between them is **0.471** against the highly expressed reference and **0.296** against the genome average — so a genome-average index would have reported about a third less of the available signal. An earlier version of this analysis used a 2012 built-in genome-average table of unrecorded provenance, which gave 0.343. All three are tabulated in `results/tables/codon_reference_comparison.csv`; the highly expressed reference is used throughout.

Three designs were generated per protein: a maximum-CAI design using the most adapted codon everywhere; a **harmonised** design sampling codons in proportion to host usage subject to design filters (no homopolymer run of six or more nucleotides, GC content between 35 and 60 per cent in every 50-nucleotide window, no internal BamHI, EcoRI, XhoI, NotI, SacI or SalI sites, no AATAAA or TATATA motifs); and a uniform-synonymous null as a negative control. The harmonised sequences are the ones proposed for synthesis and are in `results/constructs/`. All designs are deterministic given the random seed recorded in the code.

## 3.8 The titre corpus

### 3.8.1 Why it had to be built

The measurement at the centre of this thesis divides a published titre by a computed ceiling. The computed side is reproducible by anyone with the model and the sequence. The published side is not, because the numbers are scattered across three decades of literature in units that do not match, under protein names that do not match, from cultivations that are not comparable, and — as §3.8.4 shows — sometimes mean something other than what a reader assumes. No compilation adequate to the task existed for these hosts, so one was built from primary sources, and it is reported here as a result of the work rather than as its supporting material.

The corpus holds **306 records** drawn from **167 distinct publications** spanning **1990 to 2027**, covering **29 distinct protein targets** across **15 host species**. Reported titres span more than six orders of magnitude, from 0.027 mg L⁻¹ to 125 g L⁻¹.

### 3.8.2 What each record carries

Every record carries, where the source states it: the protein name as published and a canonical name; a UniProt accession where identification was unambiguous (194 of 306); source organism; host species, canonicalised host, and strain; promoter and secretion leader; gene copy number; cellular localisation of the product; cultivation mode and carbon source; the titre as reported and normalised to mg L⁻¹; the basis on which it was reported; the quantification method; volumetric productivity where derivable; year, reference, source title, DOI and PMID; a confidence grade; free-text notes; and the file-level provenance of the row.

Two fields do more work than the rest.

**The evidence quotation.** Every one of the 306 records carries a verbatim quotation of the sentence in its source that states the number. This is not decoration. A normalised figure in a spreadsheet is unfalsifiable: a reader cannot tell whether 2.5 g L⁻¹ was the product, the total extracellular protein, a cell-lysate yield or a per-gram-biomass figure converted on an assumption the compiler did not record. The quotation makes each row auditable against its source in one step, and §3.8.4 records the cases where that audit changed an answer.

**The product-class field.** Each record is classified as food protein (129), other (71), human therapeutic (61), industrial enzyme (23), food enzyme (13) or research reagent (9), and separately flagged therapeutic or not (61 against 245). The reason is that therapeutic proteins are produced under a different economic regime: a monoclonal antibody fragment worth thousands of dollars per gram justifies strain-engineering effort that a dairy protein worth tens of dollars per kilogram does not, so a correlation fitted across both classes can be reading commercial incentive rather than molecular difficulty. The classification exists so that every association reported in Chapter 6 can be, and is, refitted on the food subset alone.

### 3.8.3 Confidence grading

Records are graded **high** (207), **medium** (78), **low** (18) or **unverified** (3). A high grade requires that the titre be stated numerically in the source for a defined volumetric basis, that the host and product be identifiable, and that the quotation support the number without inference. Medium covers numbers requiring a stated unit conversion or a secondary source. Low covers numbers recoverable only approximately — read from a figure, or reported as a range. The three unverified rows are retained, flagged, and excluded from every analysis. The quantitative analyses in Chapters 5 and 6 use high-confidence secreted records only; the remainder provide context and the negative-space mapping of Chapter 7.

### 3.8.4 Hazards carried into the analysis rather than averaged away

Four hazards are documented per row rather than silently smoothed.

**Basis mismatch.** Some high reported figures are total extracellular protein, not product mass. These are flagged in `titer_basis` and excluded from utilisation calculations.

**Localisation mismatch.** Twenty-two records are intracellular and five explicitly not secreted. A secretion analysis that pools these overstates what secretion achieves. One such case was caught only by its quotation: a record entered as a secretion titre carried a sentence containing the words "cell lysis, and purification", which places the number downstream of a lysis step and therefore inside the cell. Appendix F documents the episode and what it cost.

**Non-independence.** Multi-stage optimisation results from a single study are not independent observations. Thirty-three records are flagged against an earlier row by `duplicate_of`, and study identity is retained so that heterogeneity at the study level can be modelled rather than assumed away.

**Cultivation incomparability.** Within-study flask-to-bioreactor ratios in this corpus run from roughly 8-fold to 88-fold. Cultivation mode is therefore never pooled: Chapter 6 carries an explicit fed-batch indicator and reports every association both with and without it.

### 3.8.5 Gaps, reported as gaps

Two gaps in the literature are material to the panel and are reported rather than filled by inference. No primary volumetric titre was found for β-casein or α-S2-casein in any microbial host. Secretion of ovalbumin in *K. phaffii* has been demonstrated without a stated titre. These absences are not failures of retrieval; they are the shape of the field, and Chapter 7 treats them as findings.

## 3.9 The ceiling–gap measurement

For each protein, the stoichiometric ceiling at a given fed-batch substrate loading *S* is *S* × *Y*<sub>poly</sub>, where *Y*<sub>poly</sub> is the maximum theoretical polypeptide yield on glycerol. Published fed-batch processes for this host feed of order 150–500 g L⁻¹ of glycerol plus methanol, so *S* is scanned across {150, 250, 350, 500} g L⁻¹ and results are reported as a band, with 350 g L⁻¹ as the central case. Each high-confidence titre is then expressed as a fraction of its own protein's ceiling, and the reciprocal is reported as the gap in fold.

## 3.10 Testing what the gap tracks

The design was fixed before the analysis was run.

**Response**: log₁₀ of titre in mg L⁻¹. **Inclusion**: *K. phaffii* or *P. pastoris* only; secreted product only; high-confidence records only; sequence available and verified. On the rebuilt corpus this yields **53 records over 25 distinct proteins**. **Features**: seven mechanistic predictors — log length, disulfides per 100 residues, sequons per 100 residues, disordered fraction, GRAVY, absolute mean net charge, and the maximum theoretical yield — plus a fed-batch indicator. **Model**: ridge regression with the penalty chosen by internal cross-validation on standardised features. **Validation**: leave-one-protein-out, so that all records of a protein are withheld together and the score cannot be inflated by memorising a protein from its own replicates. **Baseline**: predicting the training mean, reported beside the model, because a model that cannot beat that baseline has no skill regardless of its correlation coefficient. **Nested controls**: two further models are fitted and reported beside the full one — the process indicator alone, and the seven sequence features without it. A fed-batch run beats a shake flask by one to two orders of magnitude, so a model containing the indicator can appear predictive while carrying no information about the protein, and only the nested comparison separates the two. **Subset refit**: the corpus flags each record as a human therapeutic or not, and the whole analysis is repeated on the food and food-adjacent subset, because a therapeutic is developed under a cost structure that justifies effort no food protein can.

Univariate associations are computed per protein rather than per record, using each protein's best reported titre, to avoid weighting well-studied proteins more heavily. Because seven features are tested at a sample size of twenty-three, each association is reported with a 10,000-sample bootstrap confidence interval, a 20,000-sample permutation p-value, and a Benjamini-Hochberg q-value across the pre-specified feature set. **No claim in this thesis rests on an uncorrected p-value.**

Disulfide counts for every protein in the validation corpus are read from its UniProt feature table, as described in §3.5. The ⌊Cys/2⌋ assumption used in an earlier version of this analysis is retained in the code only as a fallback for an accession with no annotation, and `src/disulfide_sensitivity.py` reports the association under both measures on both corpora so that the effect of the change is auditable rather than asserted.

## 3.11 Metabolic engineering targets

Two analyses were run per protein. **Shadow prices** of the twenty cytosolic amino-acid pools were extracted at the production optimum and weighted by the protein's demand for each residue, giving a per-protein picture of where supply pressure falls. **Flux scanning with enforced objective function** (FSEOF; Choi et al. 2010) was run by stepping the protein demand from zero to its maximum in eleven stages while maximising growth, and retaining reactions whose flux changed monotonically; increasing reactions are amplification candidates and decreasing ones attenuation candidates. Gene associations are reported with the model's locus identifiers.

## 3.12 Techno-economic model

A transparent fed-batch model was implemented in `src/tea.py`. Every parameter is a typed object carrying a value, a unit and a **source string**, and parameters that are assumptions say so in that string rather than acquiring false authority by appearing in a table. The model computes annual production from reactor volume, working fraction, cycle time and uptime; costs are decomposed into substrate, other media, utilities (aeration, agitation and cooling power), downstream processing, annualised capital, maintenance and labour.

Two reference quantities are reported alongside the cost. The **stoichiometric floor** is the raw carbon cost of the product if the organism achieved the maximum theoretical yield computed in Chapter 4, with no cells, no losses and no equipment; nothing can be cheaper. The **engineering gap** is the ratio of modelled plant cost to that floor. One-at-a-time sensitivity (±50%) and a log-normal Monte Carlo (20,000 draws, 25% relative standard deviation) are reported.

## 3.13 Independent cross-check

Parts of this analysis were independently reimplemented from the same published model by a separate effort that had not seen this code. The two implementations agree on maximum theoretical yields to within about 1% (β-casein on glycerol: 0.6400 here against 0.636; β-lactoglobulin: 0.6535 against 0.647) and agree exactly on lactoferrin's 85.44 kDa product mass after the mass-accounting correction described in §3.4.1. That cross-check also produced the three corrections recorded in §3.3.3 and §3.4.1. Independent reimplementation is reported here as a validation step because, for a thesis whose entire evidence base is computed, it is the closest available analogue to a replicate experiment.

## 3.14 Known limitations of the method

Stated here so that the results chapters are not interrupted by them, and developed in Chapter 9.

1. Flux balance analysis predicts **capability, not realisation**. A computed ceiling says what the stoichiometry permits, not what a strain will do.
2. The model has **no secretory-pathway capacity**. Chaperone availability, endoplasmic-reticulum volume, COPII vesicle throughput and translocon occupancy are absent, which is precisely why a pathway-aware model such as pcSecYeast is the natural successor to this analysis.
4. The **codon index is genome-wide, not expression-weighted** (§3.7).
5. The titre corpus is subject to **publication bias and unequal research effort**, which is a confound the design cannot remove and which Chapter 6 argues is in fact the main finding.
6. **No experimental validation** was performed.


# Chapter 4 — The stoichiometric ceiling

This chapter establishes what the metabolism of *Komagataella phaffii* permits. Every number is a maximum: it assumes a cell that makes no biomass, wastes nothing, and diverts its entire carbon and energy budget to one protein. No strain can exceed it. The purpose of computing it is not to predict a titre but to establish a denominator.

## 4.1 Maximum theoretical yields

Table 4.1 gives the maximum theoretical polypeptide yield for each panel protein on each of three carbon sources, at the default translation cost of 4.3 ATP-equivalents per peptide bond, with the full post-translational load charged.

Two things are immediately visible, and the second is the point of the chapter.

**The protein hardly matters.** From κ-casein at the top to α-lactalbumin at the bottom, the range on glycerol is 0.612 to 0.670 g g⁻¹: a spread of **9.4%** across proteins that differ by a factor of 5.6 in chain length, by sixteen disulfide bonds, by five glycosylation sites, by thirteen phosphorylation sites and by the presence or absence of a haem cofactor. In carbon terms the convergence is tighter still: carbon yields on glycerol run from 0.829 to 0.920 C-mol per C-mol. These molecules are, from the cell's accounting point of view, nearly the same molecule.

**The carbon source matters a great deal.** Methanol yields are 30% below glycerol yields for every protein without exception (0.428–0.469 against 0.612–0.670). Glucose sits in between, about 10% below glycerol. The mechanism is in the model's own parameters: dissimilating methanol through formaldehyde to carbon dioxide to regenerate reducing power carries a growth-associated maintenance requirement of 166.8 mmol ATP per gram against 68.7 for glycerol, and the first oxidation step by alcohol oxidase passes electrons to oxygen through hydrogen peroxide, discarding their free energy entirely.

This has a direct process consequence. The AOX1 promoter, the field's standard strong inducible promoter, requires methanol. Choosing it therefore costs approximately a third of the stoichiometric ceiling before any other consideration. That is a quantified argument for the methanol-free induction systems reviewed in Chapter 2, and it is an argument that is usually made on safety and handling grounds rather than on yield.

### 4.1.1 Why the proteins converge

The convergence is not an artefact. Proteins are built from the same twenty monomers, and while those monomers differ substantially in biosynthetic cost — aromatic residues are expensive, alanine and glycine cheap — real proteins do not differ enough in composition for those differences to accumulate. β-casein is 16.7% proline and 9.6% glutamine; β-lactoglobulin is 13.6% leucine and only 4.9% proline; lactoferrin carries 4.9% cysteine against zero in β-casein and α-S1-casein. These are large compositional differences by the standards of protein chemistry. They produce a 9% difference in metabolic cost.

The two proteins at the bottom of the table, α-lactalbumin and lactoferrin, are there for the same reason: they are the two most heavily modified. Their polypeptide-basis yields are depressed by the diversion of carbon into glycan and by the oxygen consumed in disulfide formation, which §4.3 decomposes.

### 4.1.2 Sensitivity to the translation cost

The translation cost *k* is the one genuinely uncertain parameter in the construction. Scanning *k* from 2.0 to 5.5 ATP-equivalents per peptide bond moves yields by about 7% — for β-casein, from 0.669 to 0.626 g g⁻¹ — and does not change the rank order or the magnitude of the between-protein spread. No conclusion in this thesis depends on the choice.

### 4.1.3 Oxygen and carbon dioxide

At the optimum on glycerol, production of one gram of protein consumes 9.4–10.2 mmol of oxygen and releases 3.7–5.2 mmol of carbon dioxide. The oxygen figure is the one that matters industrially: it is nearly invariant across the panel, and combined with the oxygen transfer capacity of a large aerated vessel it is what sets the maximum volumetric productivity a plant can sustain. It is used in Chapter 8 to size the aeration duty.

## 4.2 Production against growth

Protein production and growth compete for the same carbon. Fixing the specific growth rate and re-maximising protein gives a linear trade-off: for β-casein, the ceiling falls from 0.640 g g⁻¹ at zero growth to 0.595 at µ = 0.05 h⁻¹, 0.550 at 0.10 h⁻¹ and 0.459 at 0.20 h⁻¹. Every panel protein behaves the same way, losing roughly 0.9 g g⁻¹ of yield per unit of specific growth rate.

The practical reading is that a decoupled process — grow the biomass, then hold it non-growing and productive — is worth up to a third of the yield relative to one in which production is coupled to fast growth. This is the stoichiometric argument for the two-phase fed-batch that the field already practises for empirical reasons.

### 4.3.1 Phosphorylation is free

α-S2-casein carries thirteen phosphoserines. Installing all thirteen costs **0.12%** of the polypeptide yield. β-casein's five cost 0.05%. The reason is trivial once stated: a phosphate group costs one ATP and adds 80 Da to a 24,000 Da molecule, so the energetic burden of casein phosphorylation is three orders of magnitude below the cost of the polypeptide it decorates.

This matters because it settles an ambiguity the field's language does not. The statement "caseins are hard because they require phosphorylation" is true, but not for the reason the phrasing implies. The host is not being asked to pay an unaffordable energetic price. It is being asked to perform a reaction for which it has no enzyme: the Golgi casein kinase FAM20C that phosphorylates the Ser-x-Glu/pSer motifs of secretory phosphoproteins has no yeast counterpart. The casein problem is **catalytic, not energetic**, and the engineering consequence follows directly — transplant a kinase, do not optimise the energy budget. A heterologous kinase expressed alongside the casein would cost the cell essentially nothing in carbon.

This is, as far as the audit in Chapter 2 could establish, the first quantification of that statement.

### 4.3.2 Disulfide bonds are nearly free, too

β-lactoglobulin's two disulfides cost nothing measurable: the oxidative folding relay consumes half a mole of oxygen per bond, which against a protein's total oxygen demand of about 10 mmol per gram is negligible. Even lactoferrin's sixteen disulfides contribute only a small part of its penalty; most of lactoferrin's 8.4% is glycan.

This is worth emphasising because Chapter 6 finds that disulfide **density is the one sequence feature that predicts titre**. Those two results are only superficially in tension. Disulfide formation is stoichiometrically cheap and mechanistically demanding: it requires correct pairing among a combinatorial number of possibilities, protein disulfide isomerase to reshuffle incorrect pairs, and residence time in the endoplasmic reticulum. The model used here has no representation of any of that. That the energetic cost is negligible while the empirical association is the strongest in the panel is therefore not a contradiction but a localisation: it places the constraint squarely in the folding machinery, which is exactly where a stoichiometric model is blind.

### 4.3.3 Glycosylation is the expensive modification

α-lactalbumin loses 8.96% and lactoferrin 8.40% of their polypeptide yield to the post-translational load, and in both cases the glycan dominates. A single core Man₈GlcNAc₂ glycan diverts eleven sugar nucleotides; the GDP-mannose and UDP-GlcNAc are made from the same hexose phosphate pool that would otherwise become protein.

### 4.3.4 Hypermannosylation, and why reporting one yield number misleads

*K. phaffii* does not stop at the core glycan. Golgi mannosyltransferases extend the outer chain, and high-mannose structures far larger than Man₉ are routine. Scanning the mannose count per glycan from 9 to 100 gives the result in Figure 4.3, which is the most consequential single number in this chapter:

| Mannose per glycan | Product mass | Mass yield (g g⁻¹) | Polypeptide yield (g g⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9 (ER core) | 16.0 kDa | 0.692 | 0.612 |

The two columns move in **opposite directions**. On a mass basis, hyperglycosylation makes the process look better: the cell converts more of its substrate into secreted material, because mannose is a cheaper, less reduced material than protein. On a polypeptide basis it is a catastrophe: a single-sequon protein loses **41%** of the protein it could otherwise have made, and the material the customer receives is more than half sugar. Bovine lactoferrin, with five sequons, behaves the same way, its polypeptide yield falling from 0.616 to 0.374 g g⁻¹.

This divergence was found because an independent reimplementation of the cost engine disagreed about the product mass of lactoferrin (§3.4.1). The disagreement was a real error, and resolving it turned a one-line result into a two-sided one. It also carries a methodological warning beyond this thesis: a techno-economic model that reports grams of product per gram of substrate without stating whether glycan counts as product can be wrong by a factor of two in the direction that flatters the process.

The engineering consequence is unambiguous and quantitative. Glyco-engineered *K. phaffii* strains, developed principally to make therapeutic glycoproteins human-compatible, are justified for food proteins on **yield grounds alone**, independent of any functional or immunological argument. For α-lactalbumin the prize is up to 41% of the attainable protein.

## 4.4 The haem cofactor

Leghemoglobin's single haem b costs 4.36% of the polypeptide yield, a figure obtained after adding an explicit mitochondrial export of protohaem IX to the model, which synthesises it only in the mitochondrion. The number is small but not negligible, and it is a lower bound: it charges only the stoichiometry of the tetrapyrrole, not the regulatory or flux limitations of a pathway that normally supplies a few cytochromes and is here asked to supply a gram per litre of product.

The published route to high leghemoglobin titre is precisely the remodelling of haem metabolism alongside enhanced globin expression. This analysis says that work is not about energy. Haem synthesis is not expensive; it is slow and tightly regulated, and that is a different problem with a different solution.

## 4.5 What this chapter establishes

1. The stoichiometric ceiling for making food protein in *K. phaffii* is 0.61–0.67 g per g of glycerol, and which of these nine proteins is being made changes that by less than 10%.
2. Methanol costs about 30% of that ceiling, which is a quantified yield argument against AOX1-driven processes.
3. Phosphorylation is energetically free, so the casein problem is a missing enzyme and not an energy budget.
4. Disulfide bonding is also energetically nearly free, which locates its known difficulty in the folding machinery rather than in metabolism.
5. Glycosylation is the one expensive modification, and under this host's native hypermannosylation it can destroy 41% of the polypeptide yield while simultaneously improving the mass yield — so the choice of metric is not cosmetic.

What the chapter cannot establish is whether any of this explains the production record. A property that varies by 9% is a poor candidate for explaining an outcome that varies by orders of magnitude. The next chapter measures that outcome.


# Chapter 5 — The gap between the ceiling and the record

Chapter 4 computed what metabolism permits. This chapter measures what has been achieved, and expresses the second as a fraction of the first. That ratio is the central quantity of this thesis.

## 5.1 Why the ratio is the right quantity

Published titres cannot be compared to one another directly. A gram per litre of a 15 kDa globin and a gram per litre of an 80 kDa glycoprotein are not the same achievement; a shake-flask result and a fed-batch bioreactor result differ by one to two orders of magnitude for reasons that have nothing to do with the protein; and a process fed 150 g L⁻¹ of substrate cannot be judged against one fed 500.

Dividing each titre by the ceiling of the very protein that was produced removes the first of these problems entirely and makes the third explicit as a scanned parameter. What remains — the fraction of its own stoichiometric limit that a process reached — is dimensionless, comparable, and bounded above by one.

### 5.2.1 The headline contrast

The contrast is stated as a distribution rather than as a ratio of extremes. A max-over-min ratio is decided by exactly two records, so it moves whenever one row is added, removed or reclassified; the extremes are retained below only as named examples.

The defensible claim is the one stated here — that metabolic stoichiometry does not bound these processes anywhere near their observed range, and that what does vary is a property of the process rather than of the protein.

### 5.2.3 Nobody is close to the ceiling

The best process in the entire corpus — a human-like collagen at 19.4 g L⁻¹ — reached **8.0%** of what the stoichiometry of its host permits. The best food protein, β-lactoglobulin at 13.88 g L⁻¹, reached 6.1%. The median protein reached 0.40%, which is a factor of 250 from its bound. This is worth stating plainly because the field's language sometimes suggests that titres are approaching a biological limit. They are not approaching it; the median published process is more than two orders of magnitude away from it, and the best is more than one.

At a loading of 150 g L⁻¹ the β-lactoglobulin result rises to 14.2% of ceiling and at 500 g L⁻¹ it falls to 4.3%, so the conclusion is not an artefact of the central loading assumption: no plausible loading brings any published process within a factor of seven of its ceiling.

### 5.2.4 Is carbon the right bound? Four alternative bounds, computed

A comparison is only as good as its denominator. If real fermentations hit an oxygen-transfer wall long before they exhaust their carbon, then measuring titres against a carbon ceiling compares them against a bound that never binds, and the gap is partly an artefact.

That alternative is computable from numbers already in hand. The model reports oxygen consumption at the production optimum as **9.4 to 10.2 mmol of O₂ per gram of product**, nearly invariant across the panel. An aerated vessel delivers oxygen at a maximum volumetric transfer rate, so over a 90-hour batch the oxygen-limited ceiling is the transfer rate times the batch time divided by that demand. Scanning the transfer rate from 50 to 300 mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹:

| OTR (mmol L⁻¹ h⁻¹) | Oxygen ceiling (g L⁻¹) | Carbon ceiling (g L⁻¹) | Binding bound |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 440–477 | 214–234 | carbon |
| 100 | 880–953 | 214–234 | carbon |
| 200 | 1,759–1,907 | 214–234 | carbon |
| 300 | 2,639–2,860 | 214–234 | carbon |

Carbon is the tighter bound in **all 36 protein-by-transfer-rate combinations**, and even at the lowest transfer rate the oxygen ceiling is 1.9-fold above the carbon ceiling. The calculation is deliberately generous to oxygen — it spends the entire oxygen budget on product and none on growth or maintenance, which no real process can do — so the conclusion is conservative in the right direction.

Oxygen transfer certainly constrains real processes through growth and maintenance demand, which this calculation excludes; what it does not do is constrain them *more tightly than carbon* at the product level.

**Nitrogen, however, does — in a batch.** The same calculation for nitrogen is less comfortable and is reported because of that. These proteins are 15.8–17.4% nitrogen by mass, so a medium supplying *N* grams of ammonium nitrogen per litre supports at most *N*/0.165 grams of protein:

| Nitrogen supplied (g L⁻¹) | Nitrogen ceiling (g L⁻¹) | Binding bound |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 12–13 | nitrogen |
| 5 | 29–32 | nitrogen |
| 10 | 58–63 | nitrogen |
| 30 | 173–190 | nitrogen |
| 60 | 345–380 | carbon |

Nitrogen binds before carbon in 36 of 45 cases and stops binding only above about 30 g of nitrogen per litre, which is far more than any batch medium for this host contains. **A batch fermentation is nitrogen-limited long before it is carbon-limited.**

Two things keep this from overturning Table 5.1, and one of them is a genuine qualification.

The qualification first: *if* a process in the corpus were nitrogen-limited at, say, 5 g N L⁻¹, its ceiling would be about 31 g L⁻¹ rather than 229, and β-lactoglobulin's 13.88 g L⁻¹ would be 45% of it rather than 6.1%. That is a different picture, and a reader is entitled to it.

What keeps the carbon bound the right one for these records is that every high-titre process in the corpus is **fed-batch**, and a fed-batch fermentation of this host titrates with ammonia or ammonium hydroxide for pH control throughout the run. Nitrogen is fed continuously and is not a fixed inventory, so it is not the operative bound for the processes being measured. The nitrogen ceiling is the bound for a *batch* process, and batch processes are not where any of these titres came from.

And the central claim does not depend on the choice at all. Nitrogen content varies by only **1.10-fold** across the panel, and the carbon ceiling by 1.31-fold. Substituting the nitrogen bound for the carbon bound therefore rescales every protein's utilisation by nearly the same factor: it moves the median but not the dispersion, and the dispersion is the argument. Whichever bound is used, the ceiling's log₁₀ SD stays near 0.03 while utilisation's stays near 0.85. The argument of §5.2.1 survives both substitutions, which is the strongest thing that can be said for it.

#### Phosphate, and the one bound that is protein-specific

Phosphorus enters the product only as phosphoserine, one atom per site, and ATP turnover consumes none of it net because the phosphate is recycled. The product's own phosphorus content is therefore the right quantity for a product-level bound, and it is the only bound in this thesis that varies strongly between proteins:

| Protein | Phosphoserines | P (% of mass) | 0.5 g P L⁻¹ | 2 g P L⁻¹ | 7.3 g P L⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| α-S2-casein | 13 | 1.65% | **30** | **121** | 441 |
| α-S1-casein | 9 | 1.21% | **41** | **165** | 602 |
| β-casein | 5 | 0.66% | **76** | 305 | 1,112 |
| κ-casein | 3 | 0.49% | **102** | 408 | 1,491 |
| Leghemoglobin C2 | 1 | 0.20% | 251 | 1,002 | 3,659 |
| Ovalbumin | 2 | 0.14% | 346 | 1,384 | 5,053 |

Ceilings in g L⁻¹; bold where phosphate is tighter than the 214–234 g L⁻¹ carbon bound. Three of the nine panel proteins — β-lactoglobulin, α-lactalbumin and lactoferrin — carry no phosphorus at all, so for them the bound does not exist.

Two things follow. **Phosphate does not bind in a standard medium.** Basal salts medium for this host is built on phosphoric acid, 26.7 mL L⁻¹ of 85% H₃PO₄ or about 7.3 g of phosphorus per litre, at which even α-S2-casein's phosphate ceiling is 441 g L⁻¹, nearly twice the carbon bound. Phosphate binds in 10 of 45 protein-by-supply combinations, all of them caseins at or below 2 g P L⁻¹.

**But it is the only bound that discriminates between proteins.** Carbon spans 1.31-fold across the panel, nitrogen 1.10-fold, oxygen essentially not at all. Phosphate spans **twelve-fold**, from α-S2-casein to ovalbumin, because it scales with a count that genuinely differs. That matters for a specific practical case: a developer reducing phosphate — to cut downstream salt load, or because high phosphate interferes with a precipitation step — is reducing the one bound that a heavily phosphorylated casein will hit first and an unphosphorylated whey protein will never hit at all. It is the only place in this analysis where the identity of the protein changes the operative constraint rather than merely scaling it.

#### Heat removal, the one check that cannot be dismissed outright

Oxygen uptake is the standard proxy for fermenter heat load, because aerobic catabolism releases a nearly substrate-independent 440–490 kJ per mole of O₂. At the oxygen demands of §5.2.4 this is **4.3–4.7 kJ per gram of product**. A jacketed production vessel with internal coils removes roughly 5–20 kW m⁻³:

| Cooling capacity (kW m⁻³) | Heat-limited ceiling (g L⁻¹) | Carbon ceiling (g L⁻¹) | Binding bound |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 344–373 | 214–234 | carbon |
| 10 | 688–746 | 214–234 | carbon |
| 20 | 1,377–1,492 | 214–234 | carbon |
| 40 | 2,754–2,984 | 214–234 | carbon |

Carbon is tighter in all 36 combinations, but the margin at the low-cooling end is **1.49-fold**, the narrowest of the three alternative bounds — oxygen's narrowest is 1.9-fold and nitrogen's is wide open once ammonia is fed.

And this calculation is **non-conservative**, which must be said plainly. It counts only the oxygen drawn by product synthesis, so it understates the heat a real process must remove and therefore overstates the heat ceiling. Growth and maintenance respiration in a high-cell-density fed-batch dominate total oxygen uptake, often by an order of magnitude, so the true heat-limited ceiling is well below the figures tabulated above and may be the tightest bound of all at a large scale with poor cooling. What this calculation establishes is the narrower claim that *the heat of product synthesis alone* does not bind. Unlike oxygen, heat removal cannot be dismissed as a candidate constraint on this evidence; it is simply not a constraint on the quantity Table 5.1 measures. Bounding it properly requires a whole-process energy balance with growth and maintenance included, which is beyond a product-level stoichiometric model and is named as such in §9.3.

### 5.2.5 The ordering is not the expected one

Chapter 2 declared the expectation in advance: the published expert judgement is that whey proteins are the most tractable targets and caseins and lactoferrin the least. The measurement partly contradicts it.

β-lactoglobulin does indeed sit near the top, at 6.1% of its ceiling and the best food protein in the corpus. But **α-lactalbumin, the other "easy" whey protein, is far down the table** at 0.31% — a factor of twenty below β-lactoglobulin, and below lactoferrin, ovalbumin and lysozyme. By stoichiometry it is an ordinary protein, 0.555 g g⁻¹ against a median of 0.646. By achievement it is in the bottom half of the corpus. Meanwhile soy leghemoglobin, which requires a haem cofactor the host must be engineered to supply, sits fifth.

Two readings are available and the data here cannot separate them. Either α-lactalbumin is genuinely difficult — four disulfides in 123 residues is a high disulfide density for a food protein — or it has received far less engineering effort than β-lactoglobulin, which has a commercial product behind it. The first reading was the more attractive one while disulfide density appeared to predict titre; Chapter 6 withdraws that finding, which leaves the effort reading carrying most of the weight. It is developed in §6.5, where it becomes the chapter's main conclusion rather than a caveat.

### 5.2.6 Food proteins and therapeutics, separated

Eleven of the 26 proteins in Table 5.1 are human therapeutics: interferons, interleukins, colony-stimulating factors, insulin, transferrin, albumin. Pooling them with food proteins is a confound rather than a convenience, because a therapeutic is developed under a cost structure and a regulatory regime that justify effort no food protein can. Reclassifying one row from one group to the other would also move a max-over-min ratio, which is a further reason not to rest the headline on one.

The corpus carries an explicit flag, so the analysis is simply repeated on the 15 food and food-adjacent proteins alone:

| Subset | *n* proteins | Median utilisation | IQR | log₁₀ SD | Ceiling span |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| All proteins | 26 | 0.40% | 0.073–2.12% | 0.851 | 1.31-fold |
| Food and food-adjacent | 15 | 0.50% | 0.117–1.22% | 0.780 | 1.31-fold |

Nothing of substance changes. The food proteins are if anything slightly *better* utilised at the median, their dispersion is almost as wide, and the ceiling span is identical because the ceiling is set by composition rather than by product category. The decomposition is therefore not an artefact of mixing two markets.

## 5.3 The caseins: the central test case

An earlier version of this analysis credited κ-casein with 1.02 g L⁻¹, but that record was **bovine casein macropeptide**, the 64-residue glycopeptide chymosin releases from κ-casein: a name-based join matched "casein" in both strings and inflated the caseins' apparent performance roughly eightfold. The corpus filter that removes fragments, fusions, isolated domains and antibody fragments is now shared between both pipelines.

A deeper re-harvest, with every record carrying a verbatim quotation from its source, then located intact casein titres where the first search had found none:

- **Bovine κ-casein in *K. phaffii*** (Iaria and co-workers, 2026): 28, 89 and 133 mg L⁻¹ in shake flask from one-, seven- and seventeen-copy strains, and an order-of-magnitude estimate of 10 mg L⁻¹ for a single-copy strain in a methanol fed-batch bioreactor at pH 3. The authors report extensive O-glycosylation and proteolysis, and note that production scales sub-linearly with copy number.
- **Bovine κ-casein in *Aspergillus oryzae***: 0.11, 0.18 and 4.14 mg L⁻¹ by ELISA, the highest of them with protease-pathway engineering and 10 g L⁻¹ yeast extract.
- **Bovine α-S1-casein in *Bacillus subtilis*** (Biermann and co-workers, 2025): 56.9 mg L⁻¹ in a genome-reduced strain, in a high-cell-density fed-batch. This is outside the two hosts of this thesis but it is the only quantitative α-S1-casein titre located anywhere.
- **α-S2-casein and β-casein**: still nothing. No primary source with a volumetric titre in any microbial host was retrieved for either.

**κ-casein has the third-highest stoichiometric ceiling of any protein in Table 5.1 and the sixth-lowest utilisation of it.** Its *Y*<sub>max</sub> is 0.657 g g⁻¹ against a corpus median of 0.646 — it is, metabolically, an unusually *cheap* protein to build, because it carries no disulfides to speak of, no N-glycans, and phosphorylation that Chapter 4 showed to be energetically free. And it reaches 0.06% of that ceiling, a gap of 1,729-fold, against β-lactoglobulin's 16-fold.

This is the thesis's central decomposition applied to the commercially most important target in animal-free dairy, and it says something specific: **the casein problem is not a cost problem.** Nothing about building a casein is expensive. What the sources themselves report is proteolysis, aberrant host O-glycosylation, and a copy-number response that saturates — failures of folding, trafficking and stability, not of carbon or ATP supply. The 2026 κ-casein study makes the point directly: *pep4* and *yps1* protease knockouts did not fully stop degradation.

Three implications follow.

First, the most commercially valuable target in animal-free dairy now has a public number, and it is two orders of magnitude below the whey protein beside it in the same host. The gap is where the engineering opportunity is.

Second, any ranking of food proteins by tractability that places caseins last is, for these hosts, now supported by data rather than by an absence of it — but the reason it is last is proteolytic and post-translational, which means the published expert ordering is right about the ranking and the mechanism both.

Third, the open ground has moved. A rigorous fed-batch titre for an intact α-S1-, α-S2- or β-casein in *K. phaffii*, with phosphorylation state characterised, is still unpublished, and remains the first item in the wet-laboratory programme proposed in Chapter 9. For κ-casein the open question is no longer whether it can be made but why it degrades.

## 5.4 What the exclusions remove

Twenty-five of 161 in-host high-confidence records were excluded as fragments, fusions, isolated domains or antibody fragments, because each reports a titre that overstates what was achieved for the protein of interest: a fragment is shorter and simpler than its parent and is often made precisely because the parent is difficult; a fusion's titre includes a partner's mass, and the partner is usually chosen because it improves secretion; and an engineered construct's sequence features belong to the construct rather than to anything in UniProt. The exclusions cost sample size and are recorded here because a reader comparing this table against the raw corpus will find the difference.

2. The ceiling's log₁₀ standard deviation is **0.029** across 26 proteins against

# Chapter 6 — What the gap tracks

Chapter 5 established a gap of one to four orders of magnitude between what metabolism permits and what has been achieved, and showed that the bound is some thirty times less dispersed than the outcome. This chapter asks what the outcome *is* explained by.

The answer is that nothing about the protein explains it. None of the seven features specified in advance is associated with titre, and the model containing them performs worse than a model containing only an indicator of how the fermentation was run. That is the chapter's result, and §§6.2–6.4 establish it.

Two candidate positive findings arose during this work and neither survived. Disulfide bond density was reported in an earlier draft at ρ = −0.438, p = 0.036; §6.2.1 records how re-reading the counts from the UniProt feature tables removed it. Mean predicted disorder was reported at ρ = −0.833 by an independent analysis of the same literature, and fell to ρ = −0.483, p = 0.117 once three faults in the underlying titre records were corrected (§6.5, Appendix F).

The design was fixed before the analysis ran and is stated in §3.10. It is repeated in outline here because the credibility of a small-sample result depends entirely on whether the analyst could have fished.

## 6.1 The dataset

Applying the inclusion rules to the rebuilt corpus gives **53 records over 25 distinct proteins**: secreted production in *K. phaffii* or *P. pastoris*, high-confidence evidence, CRC64-verified sequence available, fragments and fusions excluded. The response is log₁₀ of titre in mg L⁻¹, which spans just under four orders of magnitude.

Seven mechanistic features were specified in advance: log length, disulfides per 100 residues, N-glycosylation sequons per 100 residues, disordered fraction, GRAVY hydropathy, absolute mean net charge per residue, and the maximum theoretical yield from Chapter 4. A fed-batch indicator was included because the corpus documents within-study flask-to-bioreactor ratios of 8- to 88-fold, which would otherwise masquerade as a protein property.

Two measurement choices matter to what follows. Disulfide counts are read from the UniProtKB feature tables for all 36 accessions rather than assumed as cysteines ÷ 2, an assumption wrong for 15 of the 36 by up to eight bonds (§3.5); and disorder is measured with metapredict v3 rather than FoldIndex alone.

Univariate associations are computed **per protein**, using each protein's best reported titre, so that a well-studied protein with many records does not dominate.

## 6.3 The negative results, which are the informative ones

### 6.3.1 Metabolic cost does not predict titre

The maximum theoretical yield has essentially zero association with titre (ρ = +0.062, p = 0.770). This is the empirical counterpart of Chapter 5's narrow ceiling, obtained independently: Chapter 5 showed the bound barely varies, and Chapter 6 shows that what little variation exists carries no information about the outcome.

Together these close the question the thesis was built to ask. **The binding constraint on producing food proteins in *K. phaffii* is not the metabolic cost of building them.** This is a negative result about a widely assumed mechanism, obtained from two independent directions, and it is the most useful thing in this thesis because it tells a developer where *not* to spend.

### 6.3.2 Disorder, as pre-specified, does not predict titre

The pre-specified disorder feature — the fraction of residues scoring above 0.5 — shows no association whatsoever (ρ = −0.007, p = 0.974). This is the feature as declared in §3.10, and reported on its own terms it is a null. **It is also the wrong summary of the predictor, and §6.5 is given over to why**: the thresholded fraction is identically zero for every globular protein in the panel, so it discards the gradient that the mean score retains. The null below stands as the result for the feature as pre-specified; the mean-score result is in §6.5 and is labelled throughout as a replication of an externally generated hypothesis rather than as one of the seven.

Disorder is measured with metapredict v3, with FoldIndex retained alongside it for every protein (§3.6), so the null is not a failure of the instrument: the neural predictor separates the panel cleanly and in the expected direction — κ-casein 1.00 disordered fraction, α-S1-casein 0.63, α-S2-casein 0.58, β-casein 0.46, against 0.00 for β-lactoglobulin, α-lactalbumin, ovalbumin and leghemoglobin and 0.02 for lactoferrin. On this corpus, disorder as measured by a current neural predictor carries no information about titre.

What remains is a coverage limitation. The feature most characteristic of caseins can barely be tested on a casein: only one is in the dataset, bovine κ-casein, the most disordered protein in the corpus at a disordered fraction of 1.00, and it sits near the bottom of the table at 0.06% of its ceiling. That is one point in the direction the hypothesis predicts, and one point is an anecdote. α-S1-, α-S2- and β-casein still have no titre in these hosts, so the hypothesis remains under-tested at exactly the end of the corpus where it would be decided.

### 6.3.3 Glycosylation sequons do not predict titre

Sequon density shows nothing (ρ = +0.111, p = 0.598). Given Chapter 4's finding that glycosylation is the one expensive modification, this is worth pausing on. The likely explanation is that the metabolic cost of glycosylation, while the largest in the panel, is still only an 8–9% effect at the core-glycan level, and an 8% effect cannot be detected against a residual spread of 0.85 in log₁₀. The hypermannosylation penalty is far larger, but how heavily any given protein is hypermannosylated in any given study is not reported, so it cannot enter the model as a variable.

### 6.3.4 The food-protein subset, tested separately

## 6.4 All of the model's skill is the process term

A ridge regression over the eight predictors, cross-validated by leaving out all records of each protein together, is reported against two nested controls. The controls are the point of the exercise. A fed-batch bioreactor run beats a shake flask by one to two orders of magnitude, so a model containing a fed-batch indicator can look predictive while knowing nothing whatever about the protein.

**Table 6.3 — Leave-one-protein-out performance, 53 records over 25 proteins**

| Model | RMSE (log₁₀) | Skill vs predict-the-mean | Spearman ρ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Predict the mean (baseline) | 0.908 | — | — |
| **Process term only** (fed-batch indicator) | **0.764** | **+15.9%** | 0.207 (p = 0.136) |
| Seven sequence features + process term | 0.816 | +10.1% | 0.543 (p < 0.001) |
| **Seven sequence features, no process term** | **0.984** | **−8.3%** | 0.089 (p = 0.528) |

The ordering is unambiguous. A single binary indicator of whether the run was in a bioreactor recovers 15.9% of the error against the baseline. Adding seven mechanistic sequence features to it *reduces* performance to 10.1%. The sequence features on their own are worse than predicting the mean, by 8.3%.

The same pattern holds in the food subset: process term alone +10.6% skill, sequence features plus process term −11.4%. Being food or therapeutic does not rescue the features.

This also disposes of the rank correlation. The full model's predictions correlate with observed titre at ρ = 0.543, p < 0.001, which read on its own looks like a result. The process-only model, which contains no sequence information at all, achieves better RMSE with a *lower* rank correlation. A model can rank tolerably while predicting badly, and a rank correlation reported without a baseline is not evidence of skill. That is why the baseline row is in the table.

**The finding of this chapter is simple.** It is not that the sequence features are weak predictors; it is that they carry no signal that survives a control for how the fermentation was run, on a corpus of 53 records over 25 proteins with annotation-grade features and a pre-specified design. A residual standard deviation of 0.76 in log₁₀, after the process term is accounted for, means a typical protein's reported titre remains uncertain by nearly a factor of six for reasons the protein does not explain.

## 6.5 The disorder result, and why it is not reported as a finding

Of all the features that could explain the gap, predicted disorder is the one with a mechanism waiting for it: the caseins are disordered, the caseins are hard, and *K. phaffii* secretes into a protease-rich supernatant where a chain with no folded core has nothing to protect it. It nearly became this thesis's positive result.

An independent analysis of the same literature reported mean metapredict disorder at ρ = −0.833, permutation p = 0.00025, q = 0.008. This thesis's own pre-specified disorder feature had returned nothing, and the audit established that this was a real error on this side: the pre-specified feature was the *fraction of residues scoring above 0.5*, which is identically zero for every globular protein in the panel and so collapses twelve of fourteen proteins to one tied value. From the same predictions, the two statistics give ρ = −0.72 and ρ = −0.31. **Pre-specifying a feature does not make it the right summary of what it measures**, and that lesson stands whatever happened to the result.

What happened to the result is that it did not survive its own data. The correlation was a contrast between two clusters rather than a trend — within the globular twelve it fell to ρ = −0.455, p = 0.138 — and three faults in the underlying titre records, described in Appendix F, removed one of the two disordered proteins entirely. Corrected, the association is **ρ = −0.483, p = 0.117, with nothing surviving FDR correction**, and it has been retracted. On this thesis's corpus the comparable figure is ρ = −0.612, p = 0.060 across the ten dairy, egg and haem proteins with titres, falling to −0.116 across the full corpus.

**So there is no disorder finding.** What there is, consistently, is a negative sign — eleven fits out of eleven — of a magnitude the data cannot distinguish from chance, resting in every version on the contrast between κ-casein and everything else. One protein is not a test of a hypothesis about a protein class. The three caseins with no published titre in any microbial host, α-S1, α-S2 and β, are the three most disordered proteins in the target class, and measuring them would decide this; that is §9.5's first experimental recommendation.

## 6.6 What the variance is actually in

Section 5.2.2 gives this question a quantitative answer. A crossed random-effects model over 88 records, 30 proteins and 52 studies places 44% of the variance in log₁₀ utilisation between proteins, 16% between studies of the same protein and 40% within studies, and the mean log₁₀ spread inside a single protein is 0.638 against 0.892 corpus-wide. Adding the seven sequence features halves the between-protein component, from 0.254 to 0.127, while the residual does not move (0.232 against 0.235). A regressor that absorbs the random intercept without reducing the residual is relabelling the groups, not predicting from them — which is the same conclusion §6.4 reaches by cross-validation, in the form that a reviewer is least able to argue with.

Within that frame, three sources dominate, and none of them is a property of the molecule.

**Engineering effort.** β-lactoglobulin reaches 6.1% of its ceiling and human α-lactalbumin 0.31%, a twentyfold difference between two small whey proteins of similar size and nearly identical stoichiometric cost. The record for β-lactoglobulin comes from a study that rewired host metabolism using omics guidance; the α-lactalbumin records come from smaller studies of chaperone co-expression and CRISPR editing efficiency. These are not comparable levels of investment, and the corpus contains no measure of investment to control for. The nested-control result of §6.4 is the quantitative version of this paragraph: the one variable in the corpus that does carry information is a variable about the experiment, not about the protein.

**Strain and process.** Promoter choice, secretion leader, gene copy number, protease deletion, feeding strategy, induction temperature and dissolved-oxygen control each move titres by factors that rival the entire between-protein spread. The corpus records some of these variables sporadically and none of them completely.

**Publication bias.** Low titres are reported when a paper is about something else — a new chaperone, a new promoter, a demonstration of feasibility — and high titres when the paper is about the titre. The distribution of published numbers is not the distribution of achievable numbers.

This is a confound the design cannot remove, and it is the reason the negative result in §6.4 should be read as a statement about the literature rather than only about the model. **The published record of recombinant protein titres is, to first order, a record of how much work was done, not of how hard each protein is.**

## 6.7 Consequences

**For the field.** Comparative statements of the form "protein X is more tractable than protein Y", when supported by titres drawn from different studies, are weakly supported at best. The only clean comparisons are within-study ones, and the corpus contains few.

**For a developer.** Of the three candidate bottlenecks named in the project brief, one can be excluded with numbers and the other two can be characterised but not ranked. Metabolic cost is excluded, from two independent directions. Post-translational modification splits cleanly: phosphorylation is energetically free and is therefore a missing-enzyme problem rather than a cost problem (§4.3.1), while glycosylation is the one metabolically expensive modification and is worth up to 41% of polypeptide yield under hypermannosylation (§4.3.4). Folding remains the leading candidate for the residual, but this chapter does not establish it: the disulfide association that appeared to support it did not survive correct measurement (§6.2.1). The positive evidence for folding is now the mechanism reported in the sources themselves — the κ-casein study's proteolysis and saturating copy-number response, the host literature's response to protein disulfide isomerase overexpression — rather than anything this chapter measured.

**For the method.** The right successor to this analysis is a model that represents the secretory machinery explicitly, because that is where the evidence now points and where a stoichiometric model is blind. pcSecYeast is the obvious vehicle. This is set out in Chapter 9.

**For the thesis's own claim.** The expectation declared in Chapter 2 — that whey proteins would rank as most tractable and caseins and lactoferrin least — is not recovered. α-lactalbumin, a "tractable" whey protein, sits below lactoferrin, ovalbumin and lysozyme. That is reported as a contradiction rather than reconciled, because the declaration was made in advance precisely so that it could be.



# Chapter 7 — Where the flux would have to come from

Chapters 5 and 6 located the binding constraint outside metabolism. That does not make the metabolic analysis useless; it changes what it is for. If a strain is engineered so that folding and secretion no longer limit it, metabolism becomes the next constraint, and the model can say in advance which part of it will bind first and for which protein. This chapter reports that, for each panel protein, from two complementary analyses.

A caution applies throughout. These are predictions of *capability* from a stoichiometric model. They identify where flux would have to be redirected; they do not predict that redirecting it will raise a titre today, because today something else is limiting.

## 7.1 Amino-acid supply pressure

At the production optimum, the shadow price of each cytosolic amino-acid pool measures how much additional protein could be made per additional unit of that amino acid. Weighting each shadow price by the protein's own demand for that residue gives a per-protein picture of where supply pressure falls.

**Table 7.1 — The three amino acids under greatest demand-weighted supply pressure**

| Protein | First | Second | Third |
|---|---|---|---|
| β-lactoglobulin | Leu (13.6% of residues) | Lys | Glu |
| α-lactalbumin | Leu | Lys | Ile |
| leghemoglobin C2 | Lys | Leu | Phe |
| β-casein | **Pro (16.7%)** | Leu | Val |
| κ-casein | **Pro (11.8%)** | Tyr | Ile |

| ovalbumin | Leu | Phe | Val |

Two patterns are visible.

The first is generic: **leucine and lysine dominate almost everywhere**. Leucine is both abundant in proteins and expensive to make, and the combination puts it at the top for six of nine targets. This is a statement about protein synthesis in general rather than about food proteins specifically, and it is consistent with the amino-acid cost literature from Akashi and Gojobori onward.

The second is protein-specific and more useful: **the caseins break the pattern**. β-casein and κ-casein are the only proteins in the panel for which **proline** carries the greatest supply pressure, because they are 16.7% and 11.8% proline respectively — three to ten times the proline content of β-lactoglobulin or α-lactalbumin. α-S1-casein puts glutamate second, at 12.6% of its residues.

If an intact casein is ever produced at a titre where metabolism matters, the proline biosynthetic pathway is where the model says to look first — glutamate to glutamyl-phosphate to glutamate-5-semialdehyde to proline, through PRO1, PRO2 and PRO3 in yeast nomenclature. That prediction is specific to caseins, it falls out of composition rather than intuition, and it is directly testable by overexpressing the pathway in a casein-producing strain.

Aggregating the amplification candidates across the panel by subsystem:

| Subsystem | Amplification targets |
|---|---|
| Glycolysis / gluconeogenesis | 54 |
| Extracellular transport | 29 |

The picture is coherent and, read correctly, mildly deflationary. The amplification targets are overwhelmingly in **central carbon metabolism and amino-acid supply**, not in anything exotic. Enforcing protein production pushes flux up through glycolysis from glycerol, up through pyruvate carboxylase to replenish oxaloacetate for the aspartate family, and up through serine and glycine biosynthesis; it pulls flux down through the tricarboxylic acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, because carbon that becomes protein is carbon that is not burned.

Per-protein examples from the full table in `results/tables/fseof_targets.csv`: for α-S1-casein, enolase (ENO), phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPD), pyruvate kinase (PYK) and pyruvate carboxylase (PC, 2.3-fold) rise monotonically, while glutamate dehydrogenase (GLUDxi), mitochondrial malate dehydrogenase (MDHm), the ATP synthase and the respiratory chain fall. Gene associations are reported with the model's locus identifiers.

### 7.2.2 What this is worth, honestly

The attenuation targets outnumber the amplification targets three to one, and most of them are respiratory. The model is saying that to make more protein you should burn less carbon — which is true and not actionable, since the cell needs that respiration to make ATP for the translation it is being asked to do more of.

The amplification set is more useful but is still generic: it is largely "increase glycolytic flux and amino-acid supply", which is the standard answer for any secreted product in this host. The genuinely protein-specific signal in this chapter is the proline result of §7.1, and it is specific because it comes from composition rather than from network topology.

This is reported as a modest result rather than inflated. FSEOF on a purely metabolic model produces metabolic targets; since Chapter 6 located the constraint in the secretory machinery, the targets that would matter most are ones this model cannot generate. A proteome-constrained secretory model would generate them, and Chapter 9 names that as the priority extension.

## 7.3 A sanity check worth recording

The unconstrained maximum growth rate in these simulations, at 10 mmol glycerol gDW⁻¹ h⁻¹ with no protein production, is 0.674 h⁻¹. Reported maximum specific growth rates for *K. phaffii* on glycerol are substantially lower, around 0.2–0.3 h⁻¹. The model is therefore optimistic about growth in the same way it is optimistic about production: it reports stoichiometric capability with no kinetic, proteomic or regulatory limitation.

This is not an error, and it does not affect the yields of Chapter 4, which are computed at zero or fixed growth. But it is a useful reminder of what the whole instrument is: an upper bound. Every ceiling in this thesis is a ceiling of the same optimistic kind, which is precisely why the gaps measured against them in Chapter 5 should be read as *lower bounds on the gap*, not as precise distances.

Three class-level conclusions follow, and they are the ones worth carrying into a programme plan.

**The caseins need a protease programme, not a cost programme.** Every line of evidence points the same way: the ceiling is high, the cost of phosphorylation is negligible, and the primary sources report degradation. The remaining uncertainty is how large the protease programme has to be, and the honest answer from the literature is that the standard two knockouts are not enough.

**The whey proteins need process work, not protein work.** β-lactoglobulin is the best performer in the corpus and α-lactalbumin among the worst, from the same structural class with nearly identical ceilings. Whatever separates them is not in the molecules.

**Glycosylation is the one modification worth paying to change.** It is the only post-translational requirement in the panel with a material stoichiometric cost, the only one whose cost is computable, and the only one with a demonstrated engineering route in this host (§2.2.5). For lactoferrin specifically, the glycan question is simultaneously a yield question, a product-identity question and a regulatory question, which is a rare alignment of incentives.

## 7.5 Production white spaces, as a prospective direction

A by-product of building the corpus is a map of what has *not* been produced. These are recorded here as a prospective research direction rather than as a result of this thesis, because an absence in a literature search is weaker evidence than a measurement and because none of them strengthens the central argument.

Three classes of white space emerged.

**Panel proteins with no titre at all.** α-S2-casein and β-casein have no primary volumetric titre in any microbial host. Ovalbumin has a demonstrated secretion in *K. phaffii* with no stated titre. These are the cleanest publishable gaps in the panel, and §9.4 makes the first of them the leading item in the proposed wet-laboratory programme.

**Commercially named proteins absent from the production literature.** Several milk proteins with established food or nutraceutical interest — lactoperoxidase among them — appear in the dairy-science literature and in commercial discussion without a corresponding recombinant production record in either host. Whether that reflects technical difficulty, commercial disinterest or unpublished industrial work cannot be determined from a literature search, and the honest framing is as a question rather than an opportunity.

**Reporting white spaces rather than production white spaces.** The largest category is proteins that have been produced but whose titres were never reported in product mass, because the paper reported activity or fold-change instead. §2.3.3 argues that this is the single largest reason the corpus is smaller than the literature, and it is a white space that could be closed by re-measurement of existing strains rather than by new biology.

None of these is pursued here. They are listed because a reader planning work after this thesis will want the map, and because a map of absences is one of the few useful things a systematic corpus produces as a side-effect.


# Chapter 8 — What it costs, and what it could cost

Published cost estimates for precision-fermented protein span three orders of magnitude, from under $20 per kilogram to figures in the thousands. That range is not a disagreement about biology; it is a disagreement about assumptions that are usually not shown. This chapter builds a model in which they are all shown, and anchors it to the stoichiometry of Chapter 4 so that one end of the range becomes a hard bound rather than an opinion.

## 8.1 The two reference points

The model reports every result against two anchors.

The **stoichiometric floor** is the cost of the raw carbon alone if the organism achieved the maximum theoretical yield, with no cells, no losses, no equipment, no labour and no recovery step. At a glycerol price of $600 per tonne and a maximum theoretical yield of 0.64 g g⁻¹, that floor is

> $0.94 per kilogram of protein.

Nothing can be cheaper. Any claim of a lower production cost for a glycerol-fed process is either using a cheaper feedstock or is wrong.

The **engineering gap** is the ratio of modelled plant cost to that floor, and it is where the entire argument about the economics of this industry actually lives.

## 8.2 Baseline case

The baseline is a 200 m³ fed-batch plant at 75% working volume and 90% uptime, 90-hour cycles, a titre of 10 g L⁻¹, a realised yield of 10 g of protein per 100 g of substrate (about 16% of theoretical), 70% downstream recovery through a chromatography-free route, and $6 per kilogram of downstream processing cost. Every one of those numbers carries a source string in the code, and all of them are declared as assumptions rather than measurements.

**Table 8.1 — Baseline cost breakdown (USD per kg of product)**

| Item | $/kg | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Labour | 18.48 | 43% |
| Substrate | 8.57 | 20% |
| Downstream processing | 6.00 | 14% |
| Other media | 4.29 | 10% |
| Utilities (aeration, agitation, cooling) | 2.67 | 6% |
| Capital (15-year straight line) | 1.97 | 5% |
| Maintenance | 1.18 | 3% |
| **Total** | **43.17** | |
| *Stoichiometric floor* | *0.94* | |
| **Engineering gap** | **46×** | |

Annual output is 81 tonnes.

A Monte Carlo over all parameters (20,000 draws, log-normal, 25% relative standard deviation) gives a median of $45/kg with a 5th-to-95th-percentile range of **$25 to $93 per kilogram**. The distribution is right-skewed, which is the normal behaviour of a cost that is inversely proportional to several uncertain quantities at once.

That range brackets the published food-grade estimates for α-lactalbumin of roughly $24/kg, which is a mild validation of the parameterisation: an independently built model lands in the same place as an industry analysis without having been tuned to it.

## 8.3 What actually drives the cost

One-at-a-time sensitivity (±50% on each parameter) ranks the drivers:

| Parameter | Cost swing ($/kg) |
|---|---|
| Downstream recovery fraction | 49.56 |
| **Titre** | 38.13 |
| Working volume fraction | 28.85 |
| Plant uptime | 28.85 |
| Reactor volume | 24.64 |
| Batch time | 21.77 |
| … | |
| Substrate price | ~8 |

Three observations follow, and the first is the one that matters.

**Carbon efficiency is nearly irrelevant.** Substrate is 20% of the baseline cost and the stoichiometric floor is 2% of it. Doubling the organism's yield on substrate — an enormous biological achievement — removes about $4 from a $43 cost. Chapter 4 showed the ceiling varies by only 9% between proteins; Chapter 8 shows that even if it varied by a factor of two it would barely move the economics. **The metabolic efficiency of the cell is not where the money is.**

**Titre and recovery are where the money is.** Both act as pure divisors on the fixed costs of a batch: a batch costs roughly the same to run whether it produces 1 or 20 g L⁻¹, so the cost per kilogram falls almost in proportion to titre. The grid in Table 8.2 shows this directly.

The $20/kg commodity threshold is crossed between 20 and 40 g L⁻¹ at this plant scale. For reference, the best published food-protein titre in the corpus is 13.88 g L⁻¹ for β-lactoglobulin, and Chapter 5 showed that this is 6.1% of its stoichiometric ceiling. **The titre required for commodity economics is well within what stoichiometry permits — it is about 15% of the ceiling, not 100%.** The barrier is not thermodynamic.

**Labour dominates because the plant is small.** At 81 t/a, a $1.5M annual crew is $18/kg. At 325 t/a it is $4.6/kg. This is a scale effect, not a biology effect, and it is the clearest argument in the model for consolidation into large plants rather than distributed small ones.

## 8.4 The glycan trap in cost models

Chapter 4 showed that hypermannosylation raises the mass yield while destroying the polypeptide yield. The economic consequence is direct and, as far as the literature retrieved for this thesis shows, unremarked.

A cost model that measures product as secreted mass will record a hyperglycosylating strain as *more* carbon-efficient. A customer who buys protein will find the same material worth less per kilogram, because up to half of it is mannan. For α-lactalbumin at Man₁₀₀ the mass yield rises 14% while the protein yield falls 41%: a model built on the wrong basis would show a cost *improvement* for a strain that is in fact 41% worse.

This is a specific, checkable methodological warning for anyone building a techno-economic model of a glycoprotein in a yeast host: state the basis, and use the polypeptide basis if the product is sold on protein content.

## 8.5 Sustainability, reported as a range

Life-cycle assessments of precision-fermented dairy protein report large reductions relative to bovine milk protein — greenhouse-gas emissions commonly cited as 85–97% lower, blue water 96–99% lower, non-renewable energy 29–60% lower in the most-cited industry-commissioned assessment. Independent syntheses of many assessments give similar central tendencies with wide spreads.

Three caveats are carried rather than suppressed.

First, the result is **electricity-mix dependent**. Fermentation is an electricity-intensive process, and peer-reviewed analyses exist in which fermentation protein has *higher* emissions than efficient dairy under a carbon-intensive grid.

Second, **land-use savings are more robust than carbon savings**. Removing a ruminant from the system removes a land requirement unconditionally; it removes a carbon footprint only if the energy replacing it is clean.

Third, most of the favourable assessments are **commissioned by producers**. They are not thereby wrong, but they are not independent, and a thesis should say so.

The honest summary is that the environmental case for precision fermentation is strong on land and water, conditional on grid decarbonisation for carbon, and that the ranges should be reported as ranges.

## 8.6 What the within-protein spread means for a cost model

Section 5.2.2 reports that the mean log₁₀ spread in ceiling utilisation *within* a single protein is 0.638, against 0.892 across the whole corpus. Ovalbumin spans 200-fold across four published records of the same protein. That finding has a direct and uncomfortable consequence for any cost model of this industry, including this one.

A techno-economic model takes titre as an input. Table 8.2 shows that cost per kilogram falls almost in proportion to titre, so the titre assumption is the single most consequential number in the model after recovery. If the published titre for a given protein spans two orders of magnitude depending on whose laboratory produced it, then **the cost of producing that protein is not a property of the protein**, and a model that quotes a cost per kilogram for "recombinant ovalbumin" is quoting a cost for one process, not for a molecule.

Three practical consequences follow.

**Cost estimates should be quoted against a titre, not against a protein.** "$43/kg at 10 g L⁻¹" is a defensible statement. "$43/kg for β-lactoglobulin" is not, because it conceals the only assumption that matters. This is one explanation for the three-orders-of-magnitude spread in the published estimates that §2.7.1 describes: the models differ less in their engineering than in which published titre they picked up.

**The achievable cost is bounded by the best process, not the median one.** Since the within-protein spread is large and the mechanism is process rather than molecule, the right input to a forward-looking cost model is the best titre anyone has demonstrated for that protein, not the central tendency of the literature. Using the median under-states what is achievable by roughly the within-protein spread itself.

**Process development has a computable value, and it is large.** At 30% of theoretical yield, moving a process from 2 to 20 g L⁻¹ takes cost from $153 to $25 per kilogram. The within-protein spread in the corpus is of exactly that magnitude for several proteins. The economic value of the process-development work that separates the best published record from the worst is therefore not marginal; for ovalbumin it is the difference between a product and a laboratory curiosity.

## 8.7 How this model sits against the published ones

Four public cost models for precision-fermented protein were identified in the corpus, and trade analysis notes that none of them sits where the industry reports itself to be. It is worth stating plainly where the present model sits relative to them, because an independently built model landing in a published range is weak evidence and landing outside one is informative.

The model built here gives $43/kg at 10 g L⁻¹ with a 5th-to-95th-percentile range of $25–93/kg. The industry analysis most closely matched to product grade places food-grade α-lactalbumin near $24/kg and high-purity grades near $100/kg. The present baseline therefore sits inside that span, towards its lower-middle, and the Monte Carlo interval covers essentially all of it. That is a mild validation of the parameterisation rather than a confirmation of any number: the model was not tuned to the published figure, and it uses a chromatography-free downstream route, which is the assumption most likely to be doing the work.

Where this model differs from the published ones is in what it adds rather than what it concludes. It carries a **stoichiometric floor** — $0.94/kg of glycerol carbon at maximum theoretical yield — which turns the usual question "is this cost plausible?" into the answerable question "what is the ratio of this cost to the thermodynamic minimum?". That ratio is 46-fold at the baseline, and it is a quantity none of the retrieved public models reports. It matters because it separates two very different claims: that precision fermentation is close to a physical limit, and that it is 46 times away from one. The second is the true one, and it is considerably more optimistic.

The model's limitations are stated rather than buried. It is a single-product, single-scale, steady-state model with straight-line depreciation and no working capital, no validation or regulatory cost, no failed-batch allowance and no product-specific downstream route. It assumes a chromatography-free recovery at 70%, which for a protein requiring high purity is optimistic. It takes labour as a fixed annual crew cost independent of titre, which is right for a plant and wrong for a company. And it prices utilities from an aeration and cooling duty computed at the baseline rather than re-solved per scenario. Every one of these would move the absolute number; none of them changes the two conclusions the chapter rests on, which are that titre and recovery dominate and that carbon efficiency does not.

## 8.8 What this chapter establishes

1. The raw-carbon floor for glycerol-fed food protein is about $1/kg; the modelled plant cost is $43/kg at 10 g L⁻¹; the engineering gap is 46-fold.
2. Titre and downstream recovery dominate the cost; substrate price and carbon efficiency barely matter. This is the economic restatement of Chapter 6's finding that metabolism is not the binding constraint.
3. Commodity economics require 20–40 g L⁻¹ at this plant scale — roughly 15% of the stoichiometric ceiling, which no published process has yet reached but which is far below any thermodynamic limit.
4. Cost models of glycoproteins in yeast must state whether product means polypeptide or secreted mass, because hypermannosylation moves the two in opposite directions.


# Chapter 9 — Discussion, limitations and what to do next

## 9.1 What was found

The thesis set out to decide, for a panel of food proteins in *Komagataella phaffii*, which of the field's named bottlenecks actually binds. Six findings answer that.

1. **The stoichiometric ceiling is nearly flat.** Maximum theoretical yields span 9.4% across the nine panel proteins on glycerol, and 1.31-fold across the 26 proteins of the wider validation set, with a log₁₀ standard deviation of 0.029. The carbon source moves the ceiling by about 30%, far more than the protein does.

2. **Phosphorylation is energetically free.** Thirteen phosphoserines on α-S2-casein cost 0.12% of yield. The casein problem is the absence of a Golgi casein kinase, not the price of a phosphate group — a catalytic problem with a catalytic solution.

3. **Glycosylation is the one expensive modification, and the metric matters.** Core N-glycosylation costs 8–9% of polypeptide yield in α-lactalbumin and lactoferrin. Under this host's native hypermannosylation that rises to 41% for a single-sequon protein, while the *mass* yield simultaneously improves. Reporting one number without the other inverts the conclusion.

4. **Published titres sit 12- to 7,573-fold below their own ceilings.** No process in the corpus exceeds 8.3% of its stoichiometric limit; the best food protein reaches 6.1%.

5. **The ceiling explains none of it.** Utilisation of the ceiling has a log₁₀ standard deviation of 0.851 against the ceiling's 0.029 — the outcome is about thirty times more dispersed than the bound — and maximum theoretical yield has no univariate association with titre (ρ = +0.062, p = 0.770).

6. **None of the seven pre-specified sequence features associates with titre** — the strongest is log length at ρ = +0.232, p = 0.265 — and a single indicator of whether the run was in a bioreactor outperforms all seven combined. An earlier draft reported disulfide density at ρ = −0.438, p = 0.036; re-reading disulfide counts from the UniProt feature tables removed it (§6.2.1).

7. **No feature outside the seven carries one either.** Mean predicted disorder was reported at ρ = −0.833 by an independent analysis and falls to ρ = −0.483, p = 0.117 once three faults in the underlying titre records are corrected. Its direction is consistent across every fit and it rests, in every version, on the contrast between κ-casein and everything else. It is carried forward as the thesis's leading untested hypothesis, not as a result, and Appendix F documents the episode.

Taken together: for food proteins in this host, **metabolism is not the binding constraint, and nothing measurable about the molecule explains the published record.** The property with the best claim to an exception, chain disorder, points away from synthesis and towards survival of the product once made — but the data cannot yet distinguish that from chance. Two things follow for anyone deciding where to spend. Do not spend on carbon efficiency: the ceiling is not what is binding, and it barely varies between proteins. Do spend on titre and recovery, which Chapter 8 shows are what the economics turn on.

The third thing — where to spend inside the secretory pathway — is the one this thesis cannot settle, and the disorder episode shows why it matters. Disorder is conventionally offered as a *folding* explanation for why caseins are hard. The decomposition says the opposite about cost: a disordered chain is metabolically cheap, carries no disulfides and no glycans, and κ-casein has one of the highest ceilings in the corpus while reaching 0.06% of it. If disorder does track the gap, what it plausibly tracks is proteolytic survival in a protease-rich supernatant rather than difficulty of folding, and the one intact κ-casein study reports exactly that failure. The two readings imply different spending — improve the folding machinery, or remove proteases and shorten residence time — and separating them is the most valuable experiment the thesis can name. §9.5 states it.

The secretory pathway remains the leading candidate for the residual, on three independent grounds: the stoichiometric model is structurally blind to it, the host literature responds to protein disulfide isomerase and unfolded-protein-response engineering, and the sources behind the lowest-utilisation records in the corpus report proteolysis and saturating copy-number responses rather than substrate limitation. None of those three is a measurement made here. Chapter 9's proposed successor analysis, a proteome-constrained secretory model, is the way to make one.

## 9.2 The honest status of the negative result

The most important sentence in Chapter 6 is that **all of the model's apparent skill is the process term**. The full model recovers 10.1% of the prediction error against a predict-the-mean baseline; the fed-batch indicator on its own recovers 15.9%; the seven sequence features on their own are 8.3% *worse* than predicting the mean. It would have been easy to report the full model's rank correlation alone (ρ = 0.543, p < 0.001) and call it a predictive model of producibility. That would have been misleading, and both the baseline and the nested controls were specified to prevent it.

The right reading is that **the published record of titres is dominated by engineering effort, not by protein identity**. β-lactoglobulin reaches twenty times more of its ceiling than human α-lactalbumin, two whey proteins of similar size and almost identical stoichiometric cost, and the difference between the studies is not the molecule: one had a commercial programme and omics-guided host rewiring behind it, the others a chaperone co-expression experiment and a study whose subject was CRISPR editing efficiency.

This has a consequence for the whole comparative literature. Statements of the form "protein X is more tractable than protein Y", when supported by titres from different papers, are weakly supported. Only within-study comparisons are clean, and there are few of them.

It also means the field's standard tractability ranking — whey easy, caseins and lactoferrin hard — is not confirmed here. α-lactalbumin, a canonical "easy" protein, sits near the bottom of the achievement table. The expectation was declared in Chapter 2 before the measurement, so the contradiction is reportable rather than embarrassing.

## 9.3 Limitations

These are ordered by how much they could change a conclusion.

**The model has no secretory machinery.** iMT1026v3 represents metabolism. It has no chaperones, no endoplasmic-reticulum volume, no translocon occupancy, no COPII throughput and no protein disulfide isomerase pool. This is a severe limitation and the most consequential one, because Chapter 6 concludes that the binding constraint lives exactly there. The thesis can show that metabolism does not explain the gap; it cannot, with this instrument, show what does. The disulfide association is evidence, not proof.

**Flux balance analysis predicts capability, not realisation.** Every ceiling here is an optimistic upper bound — the unconstrained growth rate of 0.674 h⁻¹ in §7.3 is itself two to three times the experimentally reported maximum. The gaps in Chapter 5 are therefore *lower bounds* on the true gap, which strengthens rather than weakens the central claim but should not be glossed over.

**Statistical controls do not validate records.** Appendix F documents a correlation that passed a permutation test, FDR correction across 32 features, a bootstrap interval and leave-one-out validation, on data containing an intracellular yield recorded as a secretion titre, a titre borrowed from another species through an orthologue-pooling join, and a carrier construct counted as its passenger. This thesis's own filter had let the same carrier record through. The controls in this thesis are therefore not offered as a guarantee of its numbers; the verbatim quotation attached to every record is, because that is what made the fault findable.

**Sample size.** Twenty-five proteins and 53 records, which is small for a seven-feature analysis and is the reason no feature is claimed. An earlier draft did claim one, disulfide density at p = 0.036, and §6.2.1 reports in full how re-measuring that one variable from the UniProt annotations removed it. The corpus would need to be several times larger, and to carry a measure of engineering effort, before a sequence feature could be distinguished from the process term that §6.4 shows carries all of the available signal.

**The titre corpus is not a random sample.** Publication bias, unequal effort, inconsistent reporting of whether a figure is product or total extracellular protein, and the impossibility of controlling for strain and process engineering all remain. Thirteen fragments and fusions were removed; some residual contamination of this kind is likely.

**The disorder hypothesis is under-tested where it would be decided.** Disorder is measured with metapredict v3, a current neural predictor, and it separates the panel cleanly, so the null is not an artefact of the instrument. But of the four caseins — the proteins the disorder hypothesis is really about — only κ-casein has a published titre in these hosts, and one protein is an anecdote rather than a test. α-S1-, α-S2- and β-casein remain the decisive missing measurements.

**The codon index is genome-wide, not expression-weighted.** Classical CAI needs a highly expressed reference set; only a whole-genome table was reachable. This compresses the differences between designs and is why no conclusion in this thesis rests on codon adaptation.

**Sequence provenance is indirect.** UniProt was unreachable from the analysis machine, so sequences were recovered from mirrors and accepted only on exact length and mass agreement with the UniProt record, or on two-repository consensus. Every panel protein passed, but direct re-verification against UniProt is a required pre-submission step.

**Citation integrity is an open task.** A verification pass found that inferred rather than resolved DOIs were wrong roughly half the time. Re-resolution of all 306 records against Crossref and Europe PMC is in progress and must complete before submission.

**No experimental work.** Nothing here has been measured in a vessel.

## 9.4 Where the analysis could be wrong

Three ways, stated so that a reader can look for them.

**If the substrate loading assumption is badly wrong**, the absolute gaps change — but only proportionally, and the dispersion of achievement is independent of loading entirely, since loading multiplies every ceiling equally. The central claim is safe; the specific fold-gaps are not precise.

**If the disulfide association is a confound**, the mechanistic conclusion weakens. Disulfide-rich proteins in this corpus are also disproportionately human therapeutic proteins, which may have been produced under different standards and for different purposes than food enzymes. A larger corpus with a therapeutic-versus-food indicator would separate the two, and this is the first analysis to run on the merged corpus when it lands.

**If the ceiling were not the right denominator** — because real processes are oxygen-, nitrogen-, phosphate- or heat-limited before they are carbon-limited — the gap would be partly an artefact. All four alternatives are computed in §5.2.4 and carbon remains the tighter bound in every case, with the checks set up generously to the alternative so the conclusion is conservative. Three qualifications belong here. A batch process is nitrogen-limited well before it is carbon-limited, and only the continuous ammonia feeding that every fed-batch process in the corpus uses for pH control removes that bound; nitrogen content varies by just 1.10-fold across the panel, so the central contrast is unaffected, but the absolute fractions in Table 5.1 would change substantially for a batch process. Phosphate is the only bound in the thesis that varies strongly between proteins, twelve-fold against carbon's 1.31-fold, making it the one place where protein identity changes the operative constraint rather than merely scaling it. And heat is the weakest of the four checks: it leaves carbon tighter by only 1.49-fold at 5 kW m⁻³, and because the calculation excludes growth and maintenance respiration it overstates its own ceiling, so heat removal at scale stays an open question.

## 9.5 What to do next, computationally

Four of this list's original six items were completed during the work and are recorded in Appendix E: the candidate bounds are scanned (§5.2.4), the corpus is enlarged and the analyses re-run on it, disorder is measured with metapredict v3, and the codon reference is rebuilt from the host's highly expressed genes. Two remain open, in priority order.

1. **Port the analysis to a proteome-constrained secretory model.** pcSecYeast already builds protein-specific secretion reactions with post-translational requirements and makes per-protein bottleneck calls. Running this panel through it would test Chapter 6's conclusions with machinery rather than inference, and it is where the evidence now points.
2. **Model glyco-engineered strains explicitly**, since Chapter 4 values the fix at up to 41% of polypeptide yield.

## 9.6 What to do next, experimentally

The analysis is dry, and it should say precisely what would make it wet. Four experiments, each small, each testing something this thesis predicts.

**Experiment 1 — the three missing casein numbers, which are also the test of this thesis's leading hypothesis.** Express intact bovine α-S1-, α-S2- and β-casein in *K. phaffii* in one strain background under one promoter and leader, and report fed-batch titres with phosphorylation state characterised by mass spectrometry. No number exists in the literature for any of the three, in any microbial host (§5.3).

This is first on the list for two reasons rather than one. It fills the largest hole in the public record for this field: the most commercially valuable class of food protein has no published quantitative titre. And it is the decisive test of the **disorder hypothesis**, which this thesis carries as its leading untested idea precisely because the evidence for it did not survive audit.

The hypothesis is specific enough to be falsified. *Ceiling utilisation falls as mean predicted chain disorder rises, within dairy, egg and haem proteins in K. phaffii.* The sign was negative in all eleven fits run across two independently curated rosters, at ρ between −0.43 and −0.61 after the data faults of Appendix F were corrected, with p between 0.06 and 0.29 — consistently in one direction and consistently indistinguishable from chance. The reason it cannot be settled on existing data is structural, not statistical: in every version of the corpus, exactly **one** protein above a mean disorder of 0.4 has a published titre, and that protein is κ-casein. No amount of further analysis of fourteen points, twelve of which are globular proteins spanning a mean disorder range of 0.06 to 0.16, will decide a hypothesis about disordered proteins.

The three missing caseins are, by the same predictor, the three most disordered proteins in the target class — α-S1 at 0.63, α-S2 at 0.58, β at 0.46 on the mature chain. They are therefore the four-point test the literature is missing, with κ-casein's 0.86 as the fourth. The predictions are:

- If disorder tracks utilisation, all three should land below κ-casein's 0.06% of ceiling, and β-casein, the least disordered of them, should be the highest of the three.
- If disorder does not, they should scatter across the range occupied by the globular proteins, and the consistent negative sign across eleven fits was a coincidence of one extreme point.
- Either outcome is informative, and both are obtainable from one strain background and three fermentations.

The hypothesis also carries a mechanistic prediction that the same experiment can test cheaply. Chapter 4 shows a disordered casein is metabolically *cheap* — no disulfides, no N-glycans, and phosphorylation that costs nothing measurable — and κ-casein has one of the highest ceilings in the corpus. So if disorder matters it should be acting on **proteolytic survival rather than on folding**: the product should appear in the supernatant as degradation fragments rather than failing to appear at all. Running each casein in a wild-type and a protease-deficient background, and quantifying intact product against fragments by mass spectrometry rather than total secreted mass, separates the two readings. The one published intact κ-casein study already reports proteolysis that *pep4* and *yps1* knockouts did not stop, which makes this the more probable of the two and still leaves it unmeasured.

**Experiment 2 — the kinase test.** Co-express a heterologous Golgi casein kinase (FAM20C, or the bacterial PrkD/YabT kinases already shown to phosphorylate α-S1-casein in *E. coli*) alongside the casein. Chapter 4 predicts that the carbon and energy cost of doing so is negligible, so if the kinase works, titre should be essentially unaffected while phosphorylation state changes. A measurable titre penalty would falsify the "phosphorylation is free" claim.

**Experiment 3 — the disulfide test.** Produce two proteins of similar length and comparable codon optimisation but very different disulfide density — α-lactalbumin (4 bonds in 123 residues) against β-casein (none) — in the same strain background, same promoter, same leader, same process. This was proposed when Chapter 6 reported a disulfide association; that association has since been withdrawn (§6.2.1), which makes the experiment more valuable rather than less. The published record cannot separate disulfide load from engineering effort because no two records in the corpus share a strain and a process. A matched pair in one background is the only way to test the hypothesis at all, and it is one fermentation pair.

**Experiment 4 — the glyco-engineering test.** Produce α-lactalbumin in a wild-type and in an outer-chain-deficient (*och1*) background and compare polypeptide yield per gram of substrate, not secreted mass. Chapter 4 predicts a difference of up to 41% in favour of the glyco-engineered strain on a protein basis, with the mass basis moving the other way. This also tests the methodological warning of §8.4.

## 9.7 Conclusion

The field describes its central problem as folding, modification and purification, and names them together. This thesis separated them and measured what it could.

Metabolism, which is the thing a genome-scale model is best at measuring, turned out not to be the answer: it costs essentially the same to build any of these proteins, phosphorylation costs nothing, and the economics barely notice carbon efficiency. What is left — folding, secretion, recovery, and the simple fact that most of these proteins have had very little engineering effort spent on them — is where the distance between 8% of a ceiling and 100% of it actually lies.

That is a smaller claim than the project began with, and a better supported one. The useful product of a dry-lab thesis is not a prediction that something will work. It is a defensible statement about where the remaining work is, and a list of experiments that would prove it wrong.

## Appendix F — How a well-controlled correlation turned out to be an artefact

A correlation this work nearly reported is recorded here, because the way it
failed is the reason to trust the numbers that remain.

An independent analysis of the same literature, on its own panel of fourteen
dairy, egg and haem proteins, tested 32 sequence features against log₁₀ ceiling
utilisation and reported one survivor: mean metapredict disorder, Spearman
ρ = −0.833, permutation p = 0.00025 over 20,000 relabellings, Benjamini–Hochberg
q = 0.008 across all 32 features, bootstrap 95% CI −0.98 to −0.45, and
leave-one-out ρ between −0.791 and −0.929 with p never above 0.0026.

Nothing in that is wrong as arithmetic, and it is better controlled than most
published correlations of its kind. It was still an artefact, for two reasons
that no amount of statistical control could have exposed.

**The configuration was wrong for the test.** The panel held two proteins with
mean disorder near 0.87 and twelve between 0.06 and 0.16. A rank correlation
across a gap like that is a contrast between two clusters, and leave-one-out
cannot detect it, because removing either disordered protein leaves the other to
hold the contrast up. The discriminating test is whether the association
survives *within* the globular group. It did not: ρ = −0.455, p = 0.138.

**Three of the fourteen titres were not what their column said.** A lactoferrin
record of 3,500 mg L⁻¹, the panel's joint-highest, came from a construct with no
signal sequence, harvested by lysing the cells and recovering inclusion bodies
in 6 M urea: an intracellular yield. The join that picked each protein's best
record matched on protein name alone, so bovine entries borrowed human titres;
since every α-lactalbumin and osteopontin record in the corpus is human, this
removed osteopontin, one of the two proteins the correlation rested on, which
had no titre of its own. And a 5,450 mg L⁻¹ "ovalbumin" is *Coturnix coturnix*,
not *Gallus gallus* — hen ovalbumin's best species-matched secreted titre is
35 mg L⁻¹.

Corrected, the association is **ρ = −0.483, p = 0.117**, nothing survives FDR,
and it has been retracted. One protein of twelve now sits above 0.4 disorder,
which is exactly the configuration a leave-one-out cannot touch.

The same audit caught one fault in this thesis's own pipeline. It joins on
UniProt accession, so orthologue pooling cannot occur, but `src/corpus.py`
excluded fusions by the word "fusion" and let through a *Candida antarctica*
lipase B expressed on a cellobiohydrolase I **carrier**, credited with the whole
10 g L⁻¹ and sitting fourth in Table 5.1. The filter now excludes carriers,
chimeras and C-tagged constructs.

Two lessons are worth stating. A permutation p-value, FDR control, a bootstrap
interval and leave-one-out all passed on data containing a non-secretion titre, a
borrowed titre and a carrier mass: **statistical controls validate inference
from the records, not whether the records mean what their headings say.** And
the fault was findable only because every record in `titers_merged.tsv` carries a
verbatim quotation from its source — it was the words "cell lysis, and
purification" that exposed the first one. A corpus of numbers without their
sentences would have hidden it.

### G.3 What the audit changed

Two things, and both were costly to the project's original framing.

**It retired the producibility index.** The project began intending to build a sequence-derived producibility score for food proteins. The audit established that such an object is not novel, and §6.4 and §5.2.2 subsequently established that it could not have worked on this data in any case, because about half the dispersion it would have to explain occurs at fixed sequence. The second finding is the more interesting one, and it would not have been looked for if the audit had not first removed the reason to build the index.

**It narrowed the claim to the measurement and the corpus.** What remains is defensible precisely because the audit removed what was not.

### G.4 A caveat on negative searches

A search that finds nothing is weaker evidence than a search that finds something, and three specific weaknesses are recorded. Company work is not published, so a developer may have made this measurement internally. Non-English literature was not searched. And a measurement of this kind could exist inside a paper whose title and abstract are about something else, which no query over titles and abstracts would retrieve. The claim is therefore that no *retrievable published* work makes this measurement, which is the strongest claim the method supports.
